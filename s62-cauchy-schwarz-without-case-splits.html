Cauchy–Schwarz without case splits

Give two vectors a way to be paired into a scalar ⟨u,v⟩\langle u,v\rangle — symmetric, additive in each slot, pulling scalars out of each slot — and one inequality governs everything that follows from it:

⟨u,v⟩2≤⟨u,u⟩⟨v,v⟩.\langle u,v\rangle^2 \;\le\; \langle u,u\rangle\,\langle v,v\rangle .

This is the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality. It is what makes ⟨u,v⟩/(‖u‖‖v‖)\langle u,v\rangle / (\lVert u\rVert\lVert v\rVert) land between −1-1 and 11 and so deserve to be called a cosine; it is the reason the triangle inequality holds; it is the fact behind every “the projection is the closest point” argument. One wants it in the plain form written above, with no hypothesis attached, because every use quotes it in passing.

The obstacle is in the standard proof. For any scalar tt, the pairing of u−tvu - tv with itself is a quantity that cannot be negative, and expanding it gives

0≤⟨u,u⟩−2t⟨u,v⟩+t2⟨v,v⟩.0 \;\le\; \langle u,u\rangle - 2t\langle u,v\rangle + t^2\langle v,v\rangle .

Choosing t=⟨u,v⟩/⟨v,v⟩t = \langle u,v\rangle / \langle v,v\rangle — the value that makes the right-hand side as small as it can be — and simplifying yields the inequality. Every textbook does exactly this, and every textbook divides by ⟨v,v⟩\langle v,v\rangle. When vv is zero that division is meaningless, so the proof splits into two cases: the main one, and a separate remark that when v=0v = 0 both sides are zero anyway.

Splitting requires deciding “is ⟨v,v⟩\langle v,v\rangle zero or not?” For scalars that are ordinary real numbers written on paper, that question is settled by fiat. For scalars in an unspecified ordered field — and, more pointedly, for scalars that are computable reals, given by programs producing better and better approximations — it is not answerable at all: no procedure can look at such a number and report whether it is exactly zero. A proof that branches on it is not a proof of the inequality as stated; it is a proof of the inequality plus an extra assumption nobody wrote down.

So: can the inequality be proved with no side condition and no case split? It can, and the technique that replaces the division is worth knowing on its own.

Two structures, because two geometries

The pairing itself is a class with two fields, and it says nothing about signs — a pairing may perfectly well send some vector to a negative number when paired with itself (Interface/InnerProductSpace.v):

Class InnerProductSpace (F V : Type) `{VectorSpace F V} {Vi : HasInner F V} := {
  inner_linear :: forall v : V, Linear F V F (fun u => ⟨ u , v ⟩);

  symmetric : forall u v : V, ⟨ u , v ⟩ == ⟨ v , u ⟩;
}.

inner_linear says that fixing the right slot leaves a linear map V→FV \to F — which packages, in one field, that the pairing respects ==, is additive in its left slot, and pulls scalars out of it.

Leaving positivity out is deliberate. The pairing of special relativity — where the time coordinate enters with the opposite sign from the three space coordinates — is symmetric and bilinear but indefinite: a nonzero vector along a light ray pairs with itself to give exactly zero. Building that sign condition into the class above would exclude it. Instead the condition is a second class, to be required only where it is genuinely used (Interface/PosInner.v):

Module PosInner.
  Class PosInner (F V : Type) `{OrderedField F}
      {piE : EqRel V} {piZ : Zero V} {piA : HasAdd V} {piN : HasNeg V}
      {piS : HasScale F V} {piI : HasInner F V}
      {VS : VectorSpace F V} {IP : InnerProductSpace F V} := {
    nonneg   : forall u : V, 0 <= ⟨ u , u ⟩;
    definite : forall u : V, ⟨ u , u ⟩ == 0 -> u == 0
  }.
End PosInner.

The long binder block is the ambient vocabulary the two laws mention: an ordered field of scalars, a vector space, and the pairing that must already satisfy InnerProductSpace. The two laws themselves are short. PosInner.nonneg says a vector paired with itself is never negative; PosInner.definite says only the zero vector pairs with itself to zero. Wrapping the class in a Module is what lets the laws be called nonneg and definite at all without colliding with other laws of the same name elsewhere: call sites write PosInner.nonneg and PosInner.definite.

Euclidean geometry is InnerProductSpace together with PosInner; Minkowski geometry is InnerProductSpace alone. The quantity ⟨u,u⟩\langle u,u\rangle has a name of its own, norm2 u, since it appears in nearly every statement below.

Padding instead of dividing

The idea that removes the case split: never test ⟨v,v⟩\langle v,v\rangle for zero. Prove instead a slightly weaker statement, one carrying a positive slack ε\varepsilon, and then let ε\varepsilon shrink.

⟨u,v⟩2≤(⟨v,v⟩+ε)⟨u,u⟩for every ε>0.\langle u,v\rangle^2 \;\le\; \bigl(\langle v,v\rangle + \varepsilon\bigr)\,\langle u,u\rangle \qquad \text{for every } \varepsilon > 0 .

Here the divisor is ⟨v,v⟩+ε\langle v,v\rangle + \varepsilon, and it is positive no matter what vv is: PosInner.nonneg makes the first summand at least zero, ε\varepsilon is positive by assumption, and “nonnegative plus positive is positive” is a fact about ordered fields that needs no test of either summand.

Better still, with the divisor known positive the division can be cleared before it is ever written. Instead of pairing u−tvu - tv with itself at the awkward tt, pair su−⟨u,v⟩vs\,u - \langle u,v\rangle\,v with itself, where s=⟨v,v⟩+εs = \langle v,v\rangle + \varepsilon — the same vector scaled up by ss, so the same information, with no quotient in sight. Expanding that pairing (norm2_scale_sub_expand does the bilinear bookkeeping) and cancelling the strictly positive factor ss leaves the padded inequality, and nowhere did a branch appear. That is a lemma of its own (Theory/InnerGeometry.v):

  Theorem cauchy_schwarz_padded : forall (u v : V) (e : F), 0 < e ->
    ⟨ u , v ⟩ * ⟨ u , v ⟩ <= (norm2 v + e) * norm2 u.

Removing the slack is a separate, reusable fact about order: a quantity below b+εcb + \varepsilon c for every positive ε\varepsilon, with cc nonnegative, is below bb. Proved once, in Theory/Order.v:

  Lemma le_of_le_add_all_eps_mul : forall a b c : F, 0 <= c ->
    (forall eps : F, 0 < eps -> a <= b + eps * c) -> a <= b.

Statement. For all uu and vv, ⟨u,v⟩2≤⟨u,u⟩⟨v,v⟩\langle u,v\rangle^2 \le \langle u,u\rangle\,\langle v,v\rangle, with no hypothesis on either vector.

On the blackboard, the two facts meet after one rearrangement. Take a=⟨u,v⟩2a = \langle u,v\rangle^2, b=⟨u,u⟩⟨v,v⟩b = \langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle and c=⟨u,u⟩c = \langle u,u\rangle, which is nonnegative by positivity. Then for each ε\varepsilon

b+εc=⟨u,u⟩⟨v,v⟩+ε⟨u,u⟩=⟨v,v⟩⟨u,u⟩+ε⟨u,u⟩{commute the first product}=(⟨v,v⟩+ε)⟨u,u⟩{collect the common factor},b + \varepsilon c = \langle u,u\rangle\langle v,v\rangle + \varepsilon\,\langle u,u\rangle = \langle v,v\rangle\langle u,u\rangle + \varepsilon\,\langle u,u\rangle \quad\{\text{commute the first product}\} = \bigl(\langle v,v\rangle + \varepsilon\bigr)\langle u,u\rangle \quad\{\text{collect the common factor}\},

which is exactly the padded lemma’s right-hand side, so a≤b+εca \le b + \varepsilon c holds for every ε\varepsilon, and the order lemma delivers a≤ba \le b. In Coq, the same steps:

  Theorem cauchy_schwarz : forall u v : V,
    ⟨ u , v ⟩ * ⟨ u , v ⟩ <= norm2 u * norm2 v.
  Proof.
    intros u v.
    apply (le_of_le_add_all_eps_mul (⟨ u , v ⟩ * ⟨ u , v ⟩)
      (norm2 u * norm2 v) (norm2 u) (norm2_nonneg u)).
    intros e He.
    rewrite (mul_comm (norm2 u) (norm2 v)),
      <- (right_distributive (norm2 v) e (norm2 u)).
    exact (cauchy_schwarz_padded u v e He).
  Qed.

Walking it: apply (le_of_le_add_all_eps_mul ...) supplies the three scalars and the proof norm2_nonneg u that the third is nonnegative, leaving the “for every positive slack” obligation as the goal. intros e He names one such slack. The two rewrites are the two blackboard steps — mul_comm swaps the product into norm2 v * norm2 u, and right_distributive, used right to left, collects norm2 v * norm2 u + e * norm2 u into (norm2 v + e) * norm2 u. What is left is the padded lemma at that e, so exact finishes.

The resulting statement carries no side condition: not “v≠0v \ne 0” as a hypothesis, not a disjunction to dispose of afterwards. Read the type and it is the sentence from the top of this post.

What it buys: projection onto a line

The immediate use is projection. Given uu and a nonzero vv, the point of the line through vv closest to uu is projvu=⟨u,v⟩⟨v,v⟩v\mathrm{proj}_v u = \dfrac{\langle u,v\rangle}{\langle v,v\rangle}\,v, and the piece of uu left over after subtracting it is perpendicular to vv. Both halves are theorems:

  Theorem proj_orthogonal : forall u v : V,
    norm2 v =/= 0 -> orthogonal (u + - proj v u) v.

  Theorem best_approx : forall (u v : V) (c : F), norm2 v =/= 0 ->
    norm2 (u + - proj v u) <= norm2 (u + - (c *: v)).

orthogonal u v is ⟨u,v⟩=0\langle u,v\rangle = 0, and best_approx says no multiple c *: v of the axis comes closer to u than the projection does — with distance measured by norm2, which is why no square root is needed to state it.

Note the hypothesis both carry: norm2 v =/= 0, not “vv is nonzero”. This is genuinely the right condition rather than a convenience. proj_orthogonal is proved for a general InnerProductSpace, positivity or not, and in Minkowski geometry the two conditions differ: a vector along a light ray is nonzero and yet pairs with itself to give zero, so the divisor in the projection formula vanishes and there is nothing to project onto. Under PosInner the two conditions do coincide, which is norm2_neq_0_iff.

The projection is also the step Gram–Schmidt repeats: take a list of vectors, and make each new one orthogonal to all the earlier ones by subtracting its projection onto each of them in turn. That construction needs exactly what this section supplies — the residual is perpendicular to the axis — and nothing else.
