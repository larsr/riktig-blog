Backprop is associativity

A layered network is a chain of differentiable functions, f3∘f2∘f1f_3 \circ f_2 \circ f_1. The chain rule says its derivative at a point is the composite of the derivatives, and once bases are chosen each derivative is a matrix — its Jacobian — so the derivative of the whole chain is a product of matrices:

D(f3∘f2∘f1)(a)=J3⋅J2⋅J1.D(f_3 \circ f_2 \circ f_1)(a) \;=\; J_3 \cdot J_2 \cdot J_1.

Two well-known algorithms compute that product, and they differ only in how they group the multiplications.

  • Forward mode seeds a tangent vector vv at the input and pushes it through: J1vJ_1 v, then J2(J1v)J_2(J_1 v), then J3(J2(J1v))J_3(J_2(J_1 v)). The running product starts at the input-side factor and grows by extending on the left, which is the right-associated bracketing J3⋅(J2⋅J1)J_3 \cdot (J_2 \cdot J_1).
  • Reverse mode, which is backpropagation, seeds a cotangent row vector ww at the output and pulls it back: wJ3w J_3, then (wJ3)J2(w J_3) J_2, then ((wJ3)J2)J1((w J_3) J_2) J_1. The running product starts at the output-side factor and grows by extending on the right, which is the left-associated bracketing (J3⋅J2)⋅J1(J_3 \cdot J_2) \cdot J_1.

Which name goes with which bracketing is worth deriving rather than recalling: “forward” and “backward” are directions of data flow, not directions of association, and reading off the names instead of tracing the computation gets the two backwards. Trace the running product, as above, and the assignment is fixed.

Courses state this pair and move straight to the cost argument — why reverse mode is cheap when the output is a single number. The half that gets asserted rather than shown is the prior one: that the two bracketings produce the same matrix at all. That is the theorem here, and it needs almost nothing beyond a fact everybody already knows.

Two levels, one fact

Before matrices there are the maps themselves. Composition of linear maps in this development is not an abstract operation with laws to check; linear_map_comp g f is literally fun u => g (f u). So

(g∘h)∘k=g∘(h∘k)(g \circ h) \circ k \;=\; g \circ (h \circ k)

holds because both sides are the function u↦g(h(k(u)))u \mapsto g(h(k(u))) — the same term, not merely equal terms. That fact is recorded once, as the associativity law of the category of vector spaces: linear maps are its arrows, and the theorem (Theory/HomVS.v) cites the category’s law rather than reproving it:

Theorem lm_comp_associative : forall (g : LinearMap F R S) (h : LinearMap F Q R)
    (k : LinearMap F P Q),
  linear_map_comp (linear_map_comp g h) k == linear_map_comp g (linear_map_comp h k).
Proof.
  intros g h k.
  symmetry.
  exact (comp_associative
             (vectObj F P) (vectObj F Q) (vectObj F R) (vectObj F S) g h k).
Qed.

In the category instance itself (Impl/VectCat.v) the law is closed by reflexivity: equality of linear maps is pointwise, and beta reduction takes both bracketings to the same normal form.

Getting from there to matrices takes one step. A Jacobian is not a new construction: it is the matrix of the derivative in the chosen bases, so jacobian is defined as mat_of applied to the derivative map. And mat_of is a Proper morphism, mat_of_proper — equal linear maps have equal matrices. Feeding lm_comp_associative through mat_of_proper gives the theorem about actual differentiable functions, with actual is_deriv witnesses and actual Jacobians:

[[(L3L2)L1]]=[[L3(L2L1)]].[\![(L_3 L_2) L_1]\!] \;=\; [\![L_3 (L_2 L_1)]\!].

Theorem jacobian_chain3_assoc :
  forall (f1 : U -> V) (f2 : V -> W) (f3 : W -> X) (a : U)
    (L1 : LinearMap F U V) (L2 : LinearMap F V W) (L3 : LinearMap F W X)
    (P2 : Proper (equ ==> equ) f2) (P3 : Proper (equ ==> equ) f3)
    (Hd1 : is_deriv f1 a L1) (Hd2 : is_deriv f2 (f1 a) L2)
    (Hd3 : is_deriv f3 (f2 (f1 a)) L3),
  jacobian n q BU BX
    (deriv_comp f1 (fun v : V => f3 (f2 v)) a L1 (linear_map_comp L3 L2)
       (fun_comp_proper f2 f3 P2 P3) Hd1
       (deriv_comp f2 f3 (f1 a) L2 L3 P3 Hd2 Hd3))
  == jacobian n q BU BX
    (deriv_comp (fun u : U => f2 (f1 u)) f3 a (linear_map_comp L2 L1) L3 P3
       (deriv_comp f1 f2 a L1 L2 P2 Hd1 Hd2) Hd3).
Proof.
  intros f1 f2 f3 a L1 L2 L3 P2 P3 Hd1 Hd2 Hd3.
  exact (mat_of_proper BU BX (linear_map_comp (linear_map_comp L3 L2) L1)
           (linear_map_comp L3 (linear_map_comp L2 L1)) (lm_comp_associative L3 L2 L1)).
Qed.

The statement is long because it names the two computations rather than their results. deriv_comp is the chain rule: it turns derivatives of an inner and an outer function at matching points into a derivative of their composite, and it needs a Proper side condition on the outer function, which is why P2 and P3 are carried. On the left, deriv_comp is applied with f1f_1 as the inner function and f3∘f2f_3 \circ f_2 as the outer one — differentiate the tail first, so the derivative built is (L3∘L2)∘L1(L_3 \circ L_2) \circ L_1. On the right the grouping is the other way, f2∘f1f_2 \circ f_1 inner and f3f_3 outer, giving L3∘(L2∘L1)L_3 \circ (L_2 \circ L_1). fun_comp_proper supplies the side condition in the first case, where the outer function is itself a composite.

The proof ignores all of it. Everything except the two derivative maps is bookkeeping, so the proof term hands mat_of_proper those two maps and the one-line reason they are equal.

The two algorithms, by name

Two corollaries unfold each side of that equality into an explicit matrix product, so the two strategies appear under their own names: jacobian_reverse_mode identifies the left-hand side with (J3⋅J2)⋅J1(J_3 \cdot J_2) \cdot J_1, and jacobian_forward_mode identifies the right-hand side with J3⋅(J2⋅J1)J_3 \cdot (J_2 \cdot J_1), each factor being jacobian applied to the corresponding derivative witness. Both are proved from mat_of_comp applied twice, in the two orders — pure linear algebra, with no is_deriv anywhere in the argument.

Putting the two together strips the statement down to what it really is:

Theorem backprop_eq_forward_mode :
  forall (f1 : U -> V) (f2 : V -> W) (f3 : W -> X) (a : U)
    (L1 : LinearMap F U V) (L2 : LinearMap F V W) (L3 : LinearMap F W X)
    (P2 : Proper (equ ==> equ) f2) (P3 : Proper (equ ==> equ) f3)
    (Hd1 : is_deriv f1 a L1) (Hd2 : is_deriv f2 (f1 a) L2)
    (Hd3 : is_deriv f3 (f2 (f1 a)) L3),
  mat_mul (mat_mul (jacobian p q BW BX Hd3) (jacobian m p BV BW Hd2))
    (jacobian n m BU BV Hd1)
  == mat_mul (jacobian p q BW BX Hd3)
       (mat_mul (jacobian m p BV BW Hd2) (jacobian n m BU BV Hd1)).
Proof.
  intros f1 f2 f3 a L1 L2 L3 P2 P3 Hd1 Hd2 Hd3.
  exact (mat_mul_assoc (jacobian p q BW BX Hd3)
           (jacobian m p BV BW Hd2) (jacobian n m BU BV Hd1)).
Qed.

(J3⋅J2)⋅J1=J3⋅(J2⋅J1).(J_3 \cdot J_2) \cdot J_1 \;=\; J_3 \cdot (J_2 \cdot J_1).

The proof is mat_mul_assoc — associativity of matrix multiplication, which is itself one interchange of a double sum — applied to the three Jacobians. Backpropagation and forward mode agree because matrix multiplication is associative, and nothing else enters the argument.

What is not claimed

Two limits, stated rather than hidden.

The chains proved are N=3N = 3 and N=4N = 4. The four-map version, jacobian_chain4_assoc, is mat_of_proper applied to lm_comp_assoc4, which is in turn two applications of lm_comp_associative — the same move that derives general associativity from the pairwise law in any monoid, one step at a time. The general NN-ary chain is that induction carried out once more, and it is not proved. The obstacle is not the mathematics but the statement: a chain of NN steps means a dependent family of spaces indexed by the positions along it, with a vector-space structure at each and a linear map between consecutive ones, and getting “the space after step ii” to typecheck cleanly is the fussy part.

The other limit is that this theorem is about the Jacobian, the whole matrix. Real backpropagation never builds it. It composes linear-map applications directly, propagating a single vector through each layer at a cost proportional to the dimension, rather than forming and multiplying matrices at a cost proportional to its square or cube. That is precisely the cost argument the first section set aside, and proving that the vector-at-a-time computation agrees with the matrix product is a separate result about an efficient implementation of this specification.

The separation is what the theorem is for. Forward mode and reverse mode are not two computations that happen to agree; they are one computation, grouped two ways. Everything that separates them in practice — the cost, the memory, which one to use when the loss is a single number — is a question about evaluation order, asked on top of an identity that associativity has already settled.
