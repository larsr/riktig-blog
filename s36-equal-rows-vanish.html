Equal rows vanish, without counting to two

A determinant is alternating: if two rows of a square matrix are equal, the determinant is zero. Geometrically it is the statement that a parallelepiped two of whose edges coincide is flat, and it is one of the three properties that pin the determinant down.

Textbooks get it for free from antisymmetry. Swapping the two equal rows leaves the matrix unchanged, so det⁡A=−det⁡A\det A = -\det A, so 2det⁡A=02\det A = 0, so det⁡A=0\det A = 0. The last step divides by two.

That is fine over the rationals or the reals, and false in general. In a field of characteristic two, 2=02 = 0, and 2x=02x = 0 says nothing about xx. Such fields are perfectly ordinary — the two-element field is one — and the Field interface used here states no assumption about characteristic at all, so they are legal instances of every theorem below. A proof that quietly needed 2≠02 \ne 0 would be a proof that worked for some fields while claiming to work for all of them.

The gap is not an artifact of that one phrasing, either. Any argument that compares the whole permutation sum with a global reindexing of itself can only ever produce det⁡A=−det⁡A\det A = -\det A, no matter which transposition it reindexes by. To get 00 out, terms have to cancel against each other.

Two terms, same value, opposite sign

Go back to the cofactor recursion rather than the permutation sum:

det⁡A=∑i(−1)i−1Ai1det⁡(minor Ai).\det A \;=\; \sum_i (-1)^{i-1} A_{i1} \det(\text{minor } A_i).

Suppose rows kk and k+1k+1 of AA are equal — adjacent rows, for now. Look at what each term of the sum does.

  • A term that deletes some row rr outside the pair leaves both equal rows sitting inside the minor, still adjacent to each other, since nothing between them was removed. So that minor is a smaller matrix with an equal adjacent pair, and the induction hypothesis makes its determinant 00.
  • The two remaining terms delete one or the other of the equal rows. Here is the point: deleting either one of two equal adjacent rows leaves the identical matrix — the same entries in the same places, not merely a relabelling. Adjacency is what makes this true; there is nothing between the two rows whose position would shift differently depending on which one was removed. And their signs are exact opposites, because they sit at consecutive positions and sign_at alternates.

So the two surviving terms are x⋅Px \cdot P and (−x)⋅P(-x) \cdot P for the same xx and the same PP, and they cancel by the group inverse law. No factor of two is ever formed, so nothing about characteristic can enter.

  Theorem detM_alternating_adjacent : forall (n : nat) (k : Fin.t n)
      (A : Matrix (S n) (S n) F),
    (forall c : Fin.t (S n), A (fin_weaken k) c == A (Fin.FS k) c) ->
    detM A == 0.

fin_weaken k and Fin.FS k are the two ways of embedding k : Fin.t n into Fin.t (S n) — keeping its position, and shifting it by one — so together they name a neighbouring pair, and every neighbouring pair arises this way. The proof is induction on the size, splitting the sum by bigopF_extract2: a finite sum equals the sum of its values at two given distinct positions, provided every other value is zero. That lemma is stated for any commutative monoid and has nothing to do with determinants.

Dropping adjacency

The general case needs no second induction. Given equal rows ii and jj that are not neighbours, let kk be the position immediately after ii, and swap rows kk and jj. That changes the determinant only by a sign (detM_swap_general), and in the swapped matrix rows ii and kk are equal — row kk now holds what row jj held, which by hypothesis is what row ii holds — and ii and kk are adjacent by construction. So the swapped matrix has determinant 00 by the previous section, hence 0=−det⁡A0 = -\det A, hence det⁡A=0\det A = 0.

  Theorem detM_alternating : forall (N : nat) (A : Matrix N N F) (i j : Fin.t N),
    i <> j -> (forall c, A i c == A j c) -> detM A == 0.

The sign of the swap is irrelevant precisely because the value being proved is 00, and 0=−00 = -0. That is why one swap suffices here where the sign-flip theorem itself needed an induction on the distance between the two positions.

On numbers: a matrix that is not itself zero, and whose third row is independent of the repeated one, still has determinant zero, and this is checked by evaluating rather than by applying the theorem.

Example detM_alternating_Q_compute :
  detM (mat3 (1#1)%Q (2#1)%Q (3#1)%Q
             (1#1)%Q (2#1)%Q (3#1)%Q
             (7#1)%Q (8#1)%Q (10#1)%Q) == (0#1)%Q.
Proof.
  reflexivity.
Qed.

Linearity in an arbitrary row

The cofactor recursion expands along the first column, which makes the determinant’s linearity in the first row easy and its linearity in any other row awkward: relocating an overwritten row through an arbitrary deletion is the same bookkeeping problem the swap theorems had to route around.

With general row swaps available, that awkwardness disappears. To reason about row ii, exchange it with row one, use the row-one facts, and exchange back. Each side of the equation picks up the same negation, and the two cancel.

  Theorem det_rowI_add : forall (p : nat) (i : Fin.t (S p)) (A : Matrix (S p) (S p) F)
      (r1 r2 : Fin.t (S p) -> F),
    detM (upd_row A i (fun k => r1 k + r2 k))
      == detM (upd_row A i r1) + detM (upd_row A i r2).

  Theorem det_rowI_scale : forall (p : nat) (i : Fin.t (S p)) (A : Matrix (S p) (S p) F)
      (c : F) (r0 : Fin.t (S p) -> F),
    detM (upd_row A i (fun k => c * r0 k)) == c * detM (upd_row A i r0).

upd_row A i r is AA with row ii overwritten by the row function rr. Both proofs have the same three lines: split on whether ii is already the first row; if not, rewrite with det_rowI_conj — the conjugation step — at each occurrence, apply det_rowF1_add or det_rowF1_scale to the swapped matrix, and reconcile the negations that appear on both sides, by neg_add for the additive law and mul_neg_r for the homogeneous one.

The third row operation

The three classical row operations are: exchange two rows, scale a row, and add a multiple of one row to another. Each is left multiplication by an elementary matrix, and each has a determinant law. Exchange negates. Scaling by cc multiplies by cc. The third leaves the determinant alone — and that is exactly where the two results above meet:

det⁡(A with rowi+=crowj)=det⁡A+cdet⁡(A with rowi:=rowj)=det⁡A+c⋅0.\det\big(A \text{ with } \mathrm{row}_i \mathbin{+}= c\,\mathrm{row}_j\big) \;=\; \det A \;+\; c \det\big(A \text{ with } \mathrm{row}_i := \mathrm{row}_j\big) \;=\; \det A \;+\; c\cdot 0.

Additivity in row ii splits the modified determinant in two; homogeneity pulls cc out of the second piece; and that second piece has row ii literally reading row jj, so it vanishes by the alternating property.

  Theorem det_elem_add : forall (n : nat) (i j : Fin.t n) (c : F) (A : Matrix n n F),
    i <> j -> detM (mat_mul (elem_add i j c) A) == detM A.

Adding five times the last row of 1234567810\begin{smallmatrix}1&2&3\\4&5&6\\7&8&10\end{smallmatrix} to its first row gives 3642534567810\begin{smallmatrix}36&42&53\\4&5&6\\7&8&10\end{smallmatrix}, and both determinants evaluate to −3-3:

Example det_elem_add_Q_compute :
  detM (mat_mul (elem_add Fin.F1 (Fin.FS (Fin.FS Fin.F1)) (5#1)%Q) mat3_ns)
    == detM mat3_ns.
Proof.
  reflexivity.
Qed.

With that, all three row operations have their determinant law, stated for any field and any size, and the determinant is known to be multilinear in every row and to vanish on repeated rows. Those are exactly the properties a proof of det⁡(AB)=det⁡(A)det⁡(B)\det(AB) = \det(A)\det(B) runs on.
