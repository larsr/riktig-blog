Grading is four laws over one projection, and the wedge is a grade selection

Some algebras come with a notion of degree. A polynomial is a sum of monomials, one per degree, and the splitting is canonical: take the degree-kk terms and you have the degree-kk part, do it for every kk and the parts add back up to the polynomial. A multivector in a geometric algebra splits the same way: a scalar part, a vector part, a bivector part, and so on up to the dimension. The question this post answers: what are the defining properties of “splits into parts by degree” — stated so that polynomials, multivectors, and anything else that splits can satisfy them, and so that the useful consequences follow from the properties alone, never from how any one algebra computes its parts?

The answer is a single operation — the projection onto grade kk, written ⟨a⟩k\langle a \rangle_k — and four laws about it. Everything else in this post, including the geometric algebra’s wedge and dot products, is derived.

The structure

The operation first, from Structure/Graded.v — one projection per grade, with its own notation:

Class HasGrade (A : Type) := grade_part : nat -> A -> A.
Notation "⟨ a ⟩_ k" := (grade_part k a)
  (at level 0, k at level 0, format "⟨ a ⟩_ k").

The four laws. Each projection is linear; projecting twice at one grade is projecting once; projecting at one grade then another gives zero; and the parts sum back to the element:

⟨a+b⟩k=⟨a⟩k+⟨b⟩k,⟨⟨a⟩k⟩k=⟨a⟩k,⟨⟨a⟩l⟩k=0(k≠l),∑k=0n⟨a⟩k=a.\langle a + b \rangle_k = \langle a \rangle_k + \langle b \rangle_k, \qquad \big\langle \langle a \rangle_k \big\rangle_k = \langle a \rangle_k, \qquad \big\langle \langle a \rangle_l \big\rangle_k = 0 \;\; (k \neq l), \qquad \sum_{k=0}^{n} \langle a \rangle_k = a.

  Class Graded (n : nat) (F A : Type)
      {Ae : EqRel A} {Az : Zero A} {Aa : HasAdd A} {As : HasScale F A}
      {AM : Monoid A} {Ab : HasBigop A} {Ag : HasGrade A} := {
    grade_linear     :: forall k : nat, Linear F A A (grade_part k);
    grade_idempotent : forall (k : nat) (a : A), ⟨ ⟨ a ⟩_ k ⟩_ k == ⟨ a ⟩_ k;
    grade_orthogonal : forall (k l : nat) (a : A),
                         k <> l -> ⟨ ⟨ a ⟩_ l ⟩_ k == 0;
    grade_complete   : forall a : A, bigop (S n) (fun k => ⟨ a ⟩_ k) == a;
  }.

bigop (S n) f is the bounded sum f(0)+⋯+f(n)f(0) + \cdots + f(n), an operation class with defining equations of its own; the parameter n is the top grade, so the completeness law sums over the n+1n + 1 grades 00 through nn. Linear packages the first law together with homogeneity, ⟨ca⟩k=c⟨a⟩k\langle c \,a \rangle_k = c \langle a \rangle_k — the linearity post builds that class from its two one-law pieces. In the language of linear algebra the four laws say the ⟨⋅⟩k\langle \cdot \rangle_k are projections onto complementary subspaces that span everything — but here that is a consequence of the equations, not a decomposition handed in.

At top grade 3 — the algebra of three-dimensional space — the completeness law unfolds to the blackboard form, in Theory/Graded.v:

  Theorem grade_decomposition_three : forall a : A,
    a == ⟨ a ⟩_ 0 + ⟨ a ⟩_ 1 + ⟨ a ⟩_ 2 + ⟨ a ⟩_ 3.

scalar, vector, bivector, trivector. The proof peels the bounded sum four times with bigop’s equations and cleans up with the monoid laws; nothing else is available, and nothing else is needed.

The wedge and the dot are grade selections

A geometric algebra has one product. The exterior-algebra wedge x∧yx \wedge y and the interior dot x⋅yx \cdot y are not new operations to axiomatize: they are selections from the one product. For arguments of pure grade rr and ss, the product xyxy contains grades between |r−s||r-s| and r+sr+s; the wedge keeps the top and the dot keeps the bottom. Stated for all arguments by splitting into parts first:

x∧y=∑r=0n∑s=0n⟨⟨x⟩r⟨y⟩s⟩r+s,x⋅y=∑r=0n∑s=0n⟨⟨x⟩r⟨y⟩s⟩|r−s|.x \wedge y \;=\; \sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{n} \big\langle \langle x \rangle_r \, \langle y \rangle_s \big\rangle_{r+s}, \qquad x \cdot y \;=\; \sum_{r=0}^{n}\sum_{s=0}^{n} \big\langle \langle x \rangle_r \, \langle y \rangle_s \big\rangle_{|r-s|}.

These two equations are themselves a class, in Theory/Graded.v — the equations specify the operations, and any implementation that satisfies them inherits every theorem below:

  Class GradedProducts (n : nat) (F A : Type)
      {Ae : EqRel A} {Az : Zero A} {Aa : HasAdd A} {Am : HasMult A}
      {As : HasScale F A} {AM : Monoid A} {Ab : HasBigop A}
      {Ag : HasGrade A}
      {Aw : HasGradedWedge A} {Ad : HasGradedDot A} := {
    wedge_from_grade_parts : forall x y : A,
      x ∧ y == bigop (S n) (fun r => bigop (S n) (fun s =>
                 ⟨ ⟨ x ⟩_ r * ⟨ y ⟩_ s ⟩_ (r + s)));
    dot_from_grade_parts   : forall x y : A,
      x ⋅ y == bigop (S n) (fun r => bigop (S n) (fun s =>
                 ⟨ ⟨ x ⟩_ r * ⟨ y ⟩_ s ⟩_ ((r - s) + (s - r))));
  }.

One spelling note: subtraction on nat is truncated — r−sr - s is 00 when s≥rs \geq r — so (r−s)+(s−r)(r - s) + (s - r) is exactly |r−s||r - s|, with no signed integers anywhere.

The collapse theorem

Call xx homogeneous of grade rr when ⟨x⟩r=x\langle x \rangle_r = x: the element is its own grade-rr part. A homogeneous element has no other grade, and the proof is two applications of the laws — on the blackboard,

⟨x⟩k=⟨⟨x⟩r⟩k{x=⟨x⟩r}=0{orthogonality,k≠r},\langle x \rangle_k \;=\; \big\langle \langle x \rangle_r \big\rangle_k \quad \{x = \langle x \rangle_r\} \;=\; 0 \quad \{\text{orthogonality},\; k \neq r\},

and in Coq, as a calc chain (the tactic that turns a written chain of equalities into transitivity steps, from the calc post):

  Lemma homogeneous_grade_other : forall (r k : nat) (x : A),
    ⟨ x ⟩_ r == x -> k <> r -> ⟨ x ⟩_ k == 0.
  Proof.
    intros r k x Hx Hkr.
    calc (⟨ x ⟩_ k)
      == (⟨ ⟨ x ⟩_ r ⟩_ k)  { Hx }
      == (0 : A)            { (grade_orthogonal k r x Hkr) }.
  Qed.

Now take xx homogeneous of grade rr and yy of grade ss and look at the double sum defining the wedge. In every row with r′≠rr' \neq r the factor ⟨x⟩r′\langle x \rangle_{r'} is zero by the lemma just proved, and zero times anything is zero, so the whole row vanishes; in the surviving row, every column with s′≠ss' \neq s vanishes the same way. One term survives, and in it ⟨x⟩r\langle x \rangle_r and ⟨y⟩s\langle y \rangle_s can be replaced by xx and yy themselves:

x∧y=⟨xy⟩r+s.x \wedge y \;=\; \langle x y \rangle_{r+s}.

The two products differ only in which grade they keep, so the file proves the collapse once, generic in the grade bookkeeping gg (graded_double_sum_collapse, whose two support-collapse steps are bigop’s bigop_support_singleton), and instantiates it twice. The wedge case, in full from Theory/Graded.v:

  Theorem wedge_homogeneous : forall (r s : nat) (x y : A),
    (r <= n)%nat -> (s <= n)%nat -> ⟨ x ⟩_ r == x -> ⟨ y ⟩_ s == y ->
    x ∧ y == ⟨ x * y ⟩_ (r + s).
  Proof.
    intros r s x y Hr Hs Hx Hy.
    calc (x ∧ y)
      == (bigop (S n) (fun r' => bigop (S n) (fun s' =>
            ⟨ ⟨ x ⟩_ r' * ⟨ y ⟩_ s' ⟩_ (r' + s'))))
        { wedge_from_grade_parts }
      == (⟨ x * y ⟩_ (r + s))
        { (graded_double_sum_collapse (fun r' s' => (r' + s')%nat)
            r s x y Hr Hs Hx Hy) }.
  Qed.

Two steps: the defining equation, then the collapse. dot_homogeneous is the same two steps at the other grade, x⋅y=⟨xy⟩|r−s|x \cdot y = \langle x y \rangle_{|r-s|}. The hypotheses r≤nr \leq n and s≤ns \leq n are what lets the collapse find its surviving row and column inside the bounded sum.

The instance: Clifford algebras, and a lecture

The Clifford algebra implementation Cl F n represents an element as a function from basis blades to coefficients, a blade being a subset of the nn generators. The grade of a blade is how many generators it contains — bcount — and the projection keeps the coefficients of the blades at one count, from Impl/CliffordGrade.v:

  Lemma cl_grade_at : forall n (k : nat) (x : Cl F n) (D : Blade n),
    cl_grade k x D = if Nat.eqb (bcount D) k then x D else 0.
  Proof. reflexivity. Qed.

Idempotence and orthogonality are then one Nat.eqb case split per blade, completeness is the support collapse of the bounded sum at the blade’s own count, and the four laws assemble into the Cl_Graded instance. That the whole tower is reachable by instance search alone is an Example in the file — the _ asks Coq to fill in everything:

Example cl_graded_check (F : Type) `{FF : Field F} (n : nat) :
  Graded n F (Cl F n) := _.

The two products are computed by their own defining equations (cl_wedge, cl_dot — the class instance is definitional), and the payoff is the textbook identity for vectors. A vector — the embedding ga_vec of a coordinate vector — is homogeneous of grade 1 (cl_grade_one_vec), and a product of two vectors has no odd grade (cl_grade_odd_vec_mul), so of the four grade parts of uvuv only grades 0 and 2 survive, and those are exactly the collapse theorems’ dot and wedge:

uv=u⋅v+u∧v.u\,v \;=\; u \cdot v \,+\, u \wedge v.

Examples/Lecture.v walks this on one blackboard, at the concrete algebra Cl F 3 of three-dimensional space:

  Theorem lecture_graded_split : forall u v : Fin.t 3 -> F,
    ga_vec u * ga_vec v == ga_vec u ⋅ ga_vec v + ga_vec u ∧ ga_vec v.

Its proof is the four-part decomposition of uvuv, the two vanishing lemmas killing grades 1 and 3, and dot_homogeneous and wedge_homogeneous at r=s=1r = s = 1 naming what remains. The same lecture file proves the half-scaled symmetric/antisymmetric form of the split from the geometric-product post directly above it — the two roads to uv=u⋅v+u∧vuv = u \cdot v + u \wedge v, one page apart, both machine-checked.
