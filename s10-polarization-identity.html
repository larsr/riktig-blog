Inner products and geometric algebra: the polarization identity

A vector space over a field FF is a set of vectors that add — forming an abelian group — together with a scaling action a*:va *: v of scalars on vectors: scaling distributes over both kinds of sum, two scalings compose by multiplying the scalars, and the scalar 11 scales trivially. No coordinates appear anywhere. As before, == is a setoid equality: a chosen equivalence relation every operation must respect, not Coq’s equality of raw terms.

A bare vector space has no notion of length or angle. This post adds one, and then shows a single clean identity relating it to multiplication.

An inner product is a symmetric bilinear pairing

An inner product pairs two vectors and returns a scalar, written ⟨u,v⟩\langle u , v\rangle. It is symmetric (⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩\langle u,v\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle), and it is linear in its left slot: it carries a sum of vectors to a sum of scalars, pulls a scalar factor out, and respects the setoid equality ==. Because it is symmetric, the same holds on the right. The class states exactly those two things:

Class InnerProductSpace (F V : Type) `{VectorSpace F V} {Vi : HasInner F V} := {
  inner_linear   :: forall v : V, Linear F V F (fun u => ⟨ u , v ⟩);

  is_commutative :: Commutative V F inner;
}.

Definition norm2 {F V : Type} `{InnerProductSpace F V} (u : V) : F := ⟨ u , u ⟩.

The pairing itself is not a field of the class: ⟨ _ , _ ⟩ comes in as the implicit parameter Vi : HasInner F V, the one-method operational class that carries the symbol. The class states only laws.

inner_linear says: fix the right slot at any vector v, and the resulting function u↦⟨u,v⟩u \mapsto \langle u , v\rangle is a linear map V→FV \to F. Linear is the three-field class of the previous post — respects ==, additive, homogeneous — so one field here does the work of three separate laws, and every theorem already proved about linear maps applies to the pairing without restatement. The :: in front of the field registers it for instance search rather than merely storing it, so a rewrite under the brackets finds the respect law with nothing named by hand. Where a proof wants one half of linearity spelled out, Theory/InnerProductSpace.v names it:

Definition inner_left_additive (u v w : V) : ⟨ u + v , w ⟩ == ⟨ u , w ⟩ + ⟨ v , w ⟩ :=
  additive (f := fun x : V => ⟨ x , w ⟩) u v.

Definition inner_left_scale (a : F) (u v : V) : ⟨ a *: u , v ⟩ == a * ⟨ u , v ⟩ :=
  homogeneous (f := fun x : V => ⟨ x , v ⟩) a u.

Each is a definition, not a proof script: the law is already sitting in the Linear instance, and these two just give it a name at the pairing.

The last definition, norm2 u = ⟨ u , u ⟩, is the squared length of uu; write it ∥u∥2\|u\|^2. There is deliberately no square root and no claim that it is positive — positivity needs an ordered field, and we do not assume one here. norm2 is simply the quadratic form the pairing induces, and it is all the following needs.

Expanding a square: norm2_add

The one fact we will carry forward is what happens to ∥u+v∥2\|u+v\|^2. In an ordinary real inner product this is the law of cosines; here it holds formally, from the bilinearity axioms alone:

∥u+v∥2=(∥u∥2+∥v∥2)+(⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩).\|u+v\|^2 \;=\; \big(\|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2\big) + \big(\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,u\rangle\big).

The heart of it is expanding ⟨u+v,u+v⟩\langle u+v, u+v\rangle by additivity in each slot in turn:

⟨u+v,u+v⟩=⟨u,u+v⟩+⟨v,u+v⟩{additivity in the left slot}=(⟨u,u⟩+⟨u,v⟩)+(⟨v,u⟩+⟨v,v⟩){additivity in the right slot, twice}\begin{aligned} \langle u+v,u+v\rangle &= \langle u,u+v\rangle + \langle v,u+v\rangle &&\{\text{additivity in the left slot}\}\\ &= (\langle u,u\rangle+\langle u,v\rangle) + (\langle v,u\rangle+\langle v,v\rangle) &&\{\text{additivity in the right slot, twice}\} \end{aligned}

then regrouping the four terms into the two diagonal ones and the two cross ones. That is inner_expand:

Theorem inner_expand : forall u v : V,
  ⟨ u + v , u + v ⟩ == (⟨ u , u ⟩ + ⟨ v , v ⟩) + (⟨ u , v ⟩ + ⟨ v , u ⟩).
Proof.
  intros u v.
  calc (⟨ u + v , u + v ⟩)
    == (⟨ u , u + v ⟩ + ⟨ v , u + v ⟩)
      { (inner_left_additive u v (u + v)) }
    == ((⟨ u , u ⟩ + ⟨ u , v ⟩) + ⟨ v , u + v ⟩)
      { (inner_right_additive u u v) }
    == ((⟨ u , u ⟩ + ⟨ u , v ⟩) + (⟨ v , u ⟩ + ⟨ v , v ⟩))
      { (inner_right_additive v u v) }
    == ((⟨ u , u ⟩ + ⟨ v , v ⟩) + (⟨ u , v ⟩ + ⟨ v , u ⟩))
      { (add_rotate ⟨ u , u ⟩ ⟨ u , v ⟩ ⟨ v , u ⟩ ⟨ v , v ⟩) }.
Qed.

Reading the chain:

  • inner_left_additive u v (u + v) splits the left slot: ⟨u+v,w⟩=⟨u,w⟩+⟨v,w⟩\langle u+v, w\rangle = \langle u,w\rangle + \langle v,w\rangle, with w=u+vw = u+v.
  • inner_right_additive u u v splits the right slot of the first term. This right-additivity is not an axiom — it was derived earlier in the file from symmetry plus left-additivity — but it is available now as an ordinary lemma.
  • inner_right_additive v u v splits the right slot of the second term.
  • add_rotate is a pure abelian-group rearrangement, (a+b)+(c+d)=(a+d)+(b+c)(a+b)+(c+d) = (a+d)+(b+c), proved back in the abelian-groups post. It gathers the two diagonal scalars ⟨u,u⟩,⟨v,v⟩\langle u,u\rangle, \langle v,v\rangle into one pair and the two cross scalars ⟨u,v⟩,⟨v,u⟩\langle u,v\rangle, \langle v,u\rangle into the other.

Since ∥w∥2\|w\|^2 is defined as ⟨w,w⟩\langle w,w\rangle, renaming the three diagonal pairings gives the statement we wanted:

Theorem norm2_add : forall u v : V,
  norm2 (u + v) == (norm2 u + norm2 v) + (⟨ u , v ⟩ + ⟨ v , u ⟩).

Notice the cross term ⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,u\rangle. It is the whole subject of the rest of this post.

A squared length without a pairing: quadratic forms

Look again at what the cross term is made of. The quantity ∥u∥2\|u\|^2 never asks the pairing about two different vectors — it only ever feeds one vector to both slots. So a structure that wants nothing but squared lengths does not need a pairing at all; it needs a function Q:V→FQ : V \to F that behaves like one. Such a function is a quadratic form, and three conditions pin the behaviour down:

  • it respects ==;
  • scaling a vector by aa scales its value by a2a^2: Q(au)=a2Q(u)Q(a\,u) = a^2\,Q(u);
  • its polar form, the correction term polar⁡Q(u,v)=Q(u+v)−Q(u)−Q(v)\operatorname{polar} Q(u,v) = Q(u+v) - Q(u) - Q(v) measuring how far QQ is from additive, is linear in uu for each fixed vv.

The third condition is the one doing the real work: it is exactly the statement that QQ is a degree-two function rather than some arbitrary one. In Interface/QuadraticForm.v:

Definition polar {F V : Type} `{VectorSpace F V} (Q : V -> F) (u v : V) : F :=
  Q (u + v) + - Q u + - Q v.

Class QuadraticForm (F V : Type) `{VectorSpace F V} (Q : V -> F) := {
  quad_proper  :: Proper (equ ==> equ) Q;
  quad_scale   :: Homogeneous F V F mult (fun a => a * a) Q;
  polar_linear :: forall v : V, Linear F V F (fun u => polar Q u v);
}.

Homogeneous is the same class the linear maps used, with the weight applied to the scalar left as a parameter: Homogeneous F V F mult (fun a => a * a) Q unfolds to Q (a *: u) == (a * a) * Q u. A linear map passes fun a => a there; a quadratic form passes the squaring.

Every inner product produces a quadratic form — take Q=∥⋅∥2Q = \|\cdot\|^2 — and its polar form is the cross term of norm2_add, doubled up by symmetry (Theory/QuadraticForm.v):

Lemma polar_norm2 : forall u v : V, polar norm2 u v == ⟨ u , v ⟩ + ⟨ u , v ⟩.
Proof.
  intros u v. unfold polar.
  rewrite (norm2_add u v), <- (commutative u v). ring.
Qed.

norm2_add turns the goal’s ∥u+v∥2\|u+v\|^2 into (∥u∥2+∥v∥2)+(⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩)(\|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2) + (\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,u\rangle); commutative, used right-to-left, rewrites the ⟨v,u⟩\langle v,u\rangle into a second ⟨u,v⟩\langle u,v\rangle. What is left is an identity in the abelian group with ∥u∥2\|u\|^2, ∥v∥2\|v\|^2 and ⟨u,v⟩\langle u,v\rangle as opaque atoms — the two squared lengths cancel against the two subtractions, leaving the doubled cross term — so ring finishes it without being told which lemma to use. That instance is Inner_QuadraticForm, and it is what connects the second half of this post back to the first.

Geometric algebra: a vector squared is its own value under QQ

Now embed the vectors into a ring. A ring AA has an addition and a multiplication with a unit 11, the multiplication distributing over the addition on both sides — and, crucially, it need not be commutative (the endomorphism ring of the previous post was the first example). A geometric algebra over (V,Q)(V, Q) is such a ring AA, with VV mapped into it linearly by a map ga_vec, subject to one governing axiom — writing eue_u for the image of the vector uu in the ring:

eueu=Q(u)*:1.e_u\, e_u \;=\; Q(u) \mathbin{*:} 1.

In words: a vector, multiplied by itself in the ring, is its own squared length — as a scalar multiple of the ring’s unit. That axiom, GeometricAlgebra.contraction, is the seed of the Clifford algebras, the setting in which rotations and reflections turn into ordinary multiplication.

Class GeometricAlgebra (F V A : Type) `{QuadraticForm F V}
    {Ae : EqRel A} {Az : Zero A} {Aa : HasAdd A} {An : HasNeg A}
    {Ao : One A} {Am : HasMult A} {As : HasScale F A} := {
  ring    :: Ring A;
  scaling :: ScaleLaws F A;

  mul_linear_l :: forall y : A, Linear F A A (fun x => x * y);
  mul_linear_r :: forall x : A, Linear F A A (fun y => x * y);

  ga_vec     : V -> A;
  vec_linear :: Linear F V A ga_vec;

  contraction : forall u : V, ga_vec u * ga_vec u == Q u *: 1;
}.

Seven fields, and five of them are structures already defined elsewhere. ring :: Ring A does not restate associativity, distributivity and the rest — it is the ring, held as a field and declared ::, so instance search finds it and every ring theorem in the library applies to A at once. scaling :: ScaleLaws F A holds the module laws for *: the same way. The two Linear fields say multiplication is linear in each argument separately, which is what makes the scalars pass through products; vec_linear says the embedding is linear. Only contraction is new mathematics, and it is one line.

The contraction axiom only ever mentions a vector squared — a vector times itself. What does it say about the product of two different vectors? That is what the polarization identity extracts.

The polarization identity

The claim, in one sentence: the symmetric part of the geometric product of two vectors — euev+eveue_u e_v + e_v e_u — is the polar form of QQ:

euev+eveu=polar⁡Q(u,v)*:1.e_u\, e_v + e_v\, e_u \;=\; \operatorname{polar} Q(u,v) \mathbin{*:} 1.

The blackboard argument is to square u+vu+v in two ways and equate them. Expanding the product in the ring, then contracting each squared term:

(eu+ev)(eu+ev)=eueu+eveu+euev+evev{distributivity}=Q(u)*:1+Q(v)*:1+(euev+eveu){contraction on each square}\begin{aligned} (e_u + e_v)(e_u + e_v) &= e_u e_u + e_v e_u + e_u e_v + e_v e_v &&\{\text{distributivity}\}\\ &= Q(u) \mathbin{*:} 1 + Q(v) \mathbin{*:} 1 + (e_u e_v + e_v e_u) &&\{\text{contraction on each square}\} \end{aligned}

But eu+ev=eu+ve_u + e_v = e_{u+v} because the embedding is linear, so the same quantity contracts directly:

(eu+ev)(eu+ev)=eu+veu+v{linearity of the embedding}=Q(u+v)*:1{contraction}(e_u + e_v)(e_u + e_v) = e_{u+v}\, e_{u+v} \quad\{\text{linearity of the embedding}\}\quad = Q(u+v) \mathbin{*:} 1 \quad\{\text{contraction}\}

Equate the two, and use the definition of the polar form — Q(u+v)=(Q(u)+Q(v))+polar⁡Q(u,v)Q(u+v) = \big(Q(u) + Q(v)\big) + \operatorname{polar} Q(u,v):

Q(u)*:1+Q(v)*:1+(euev+eveu)=(Q(u)+Q(v)+polar⁡Q(u,v))*:1.Q(u) \mathbin{*:} 1 + Q(v) \mathbin{*:} 1 + (e_u e_v + e_v e_u) = \big(Q(u) + Q(v) + \operatorname{polar} Q(u,v)\big) \mathbin{*:} 1.

The two QQ terms are identical on both sides. Cancel them, and what is left is precisely the identity.

In Coq this is two lemmas. The first, contract_add, is the “square in two ways and equate” step: it drives the two contracted terms plus the cross terms all the way to Q(u+v)*:1Q(u+v) *: 1.

Lemma contract_add : forall u v : V,
  (Q u *: (1 : A) + Q v *: 1)
    + (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)
  == Q (u + v) *: 1.
Proof.
  intros u v.
  calc (((Q u *: (1 : A) + Q v *: (1 : A))
          + (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)) : A)
    == (((ga_vec u * ga_vec u + Q v *: (1 : A))
          + (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)) : A)
      { (GeometricAlgebra.contraction u) }
    == (((ga_vec u * ga_vec u + ga_vec v * ga_vec v)
          + (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)) : A)
      { (GeometricAlgebra.contraction v) }
    == (((ga_vec u * ga_vec u + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)
          + (ga_vec v * ga_vec v + ga_vec u * ga_vec v)) : A)
      { (add_rotate (ga_vec u * ga_vec u) (ga_vec v * ga_vec v)
           (ga_vec u * ga_vec v) (ga_vec v * ga_vec u)) }
    == (((ga_vec u * ga_vec u + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)
          + (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec v)) : A)
      { (commutative (ga_vec v * ga_vec v) (ga_vec u * ga_vec v)) }
    == (((ga_vec u + ga_vec v) * (ga_vec u + ga_vec v)) : A)
      { (mul_add_expand (ga_vec u) (ga_vec v) (ga_vec u) (ga_vec v)) }
    == ((ga_vec (u + v) * ga_vec (u + v)) : A)
      { ((fun x y => additive (opA := add) (opB := add) (f := ga_vec) x y) u v) }
    == ((Q (u + v) *: (1 : A)) : A)
      { (GeometricAlgebra.contraction (u + v)) }.
Qed.
  • GeometricAlgebra.contraction u, used right-to-left, turns Q(u)*:1Q(u) *: 1 back into eueue_u e_u; GeometricAlgebra.contraction v does the same for Q(v)*:1Q(v) *: 1. Now every term is a product of embedded vectors.
  • add_rotate, the abelian-group rearrangement from the abelian-groups post, and commutative, commutativity of +, shuffle the four products into the pattern (eueu+eveu)+(euev+evev)(e_u e_u + e_v e_u) + (e_u e_v + e_v e_v).
  • mul_add_expand is the ring’s own expansion (a+b)(c+d)=(ac+bc)+(ad+bd)(a+b)(c+d) = (ac+bc) + (ad+bd), used right-to-left to fold those four products back into the single square (eu+ev)(eu+ev)(e_u + e_v)(e_u + e_v).
  • The next step replaces eu+eve_u + e_v by eu+ve_{u+v}. Its justification is additive (f := ga_vec), the additivity half of the vec_linear field, fetched from the instance database by instance search rather than named as a field — the opA := add and opB := add annotations just tell search which two operations are meant.
  • GeometricAlgebra.contraction (u+v) contracts that square to Q(u+v)*:1Q(u+v) *: 1.

The second lemma, polarization, performs the cancellation. First a one-line rearrangement of the definition of polar, then the proof:

Lemma polar_recovers : forall u v : V, (Q u + Q v) + polar Q u v == Q (u + v).
Proof.
  intros u v. unfold polar.
  rewrite <- (neg_add_assoc (Q (u + v)) (Q u) (Q v)).
  exact (add_sub_cancel (Q u + Q v) (Q (u + v))).
Qed.

Theorem polarization : forall u v : V,
  ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u == polar Q u v *: (1 : A).
Proof.
  intros u v.
  apply (cancel_l (Q u *: (1 : A) + Q v *: 1)).
  transitivity (Q (u + v) *: (1 : A)).
  { exact (contract_add u v). }
  transitivity (((Q u + Q v) + polar Q u v) *: (1 : A)).
  { exact (ScaleLaws.scale_proper _ _ (symmetry (polar_recovers u v))
             (1 : A) (1 : A) (reflexivity (1 : A))). }
  transitivity ((Q u + Q v) *: (1 : A) + polar Q u v *: (1 : A)).
  { exact (right_distributive (Q u + Q v) (polar Q u v) (1 : A)). }
  exact (Magma.op_proper _ _ (right_distributive (Q u) (Q v) (1 : A))
           _ _ (reflexivity (polar Q u v *: (1 : A)))).
Qed.
  • apply (cancel_l (Q u *: 1 + Q v *: 1)) is the cancellation step made precise. cancel_l — “if a+b=a+ca + b = a + c then b=cb = c” — was proved back in the groups post; applying it here adds the two contracted terms to both sides of the goal, so it now suffices to prove the augmented equation.
  • The first transitivity step is contract_add: the left side, the contracted terms plus the cross terms, becomes Q(u+v)*:1Q(u+v) *: 1.
  • The second unfolds Q(u+v)Q(u+v) back into (Q(u)+Q(v))+polar⁡Q(u,v)(Q(u) + Q(v)) + \operatorname{polar} Q(u,v) by polar_recovers, read backwards. It cannot be a bare rewrite, because the rewriting happens under *:; ScaleLaws.scale_proper is the law saying *: respects == in each argument, and applying it to the two equations — the interesting one on the left, reflexivity on the right — performs the substitution.
  • The last two steps are right_distributive for *:, splitting a scaling by a sum of scalars into a sum of scalings, applied twice: once at the top and once inside the left summand, the second time under + and so again through a respect law, Magma.op_proper. What is left matches what cancel_l put on the right, and the goal closes.

Now specialize. Take Q=∥⋅∥2Q = \|\cdot\|^2 coming from an inner product — that is the Inner_QuadraticForm instance — and polar_norm2 says polar⁡Q(u,v)=⟨u,v⟩+⟨u,v⟩\operatorname{polar} Q(u,v) = \langle u,v\rangle + \langle u,v\rangle. So for an inner product space the identity reads

euev+eveu=(⟨u,v⟩+⟨u,v⟩)*:1,e_u e_v + e_v e_u = \big(\langle u,v\rangle + \langle u,v\rangle\big) \mathbin{*:} 1,

the classical polarization identity: the symmetric part of the geometric product is twice the inner product. Stating the theorem over a quadratic form rather than a pairing costs nothing here — the proof never used symmetry or two-slot additivity — and it buys the forms that no inner product gives, such as the indefinite ones of spacetime.

An algebra that satisfies the axioms

The axioms are not vacuous: Impl/CliffordFin.v constructs instances. The simplest interesting one takes the vector space to be the field itself — a one-dimensional line — with the negated square as its quadratic form, Q(u)=−(uu)Q(u) = -(u\,u), and takes the ring AA to be the complex numbers over FF (pairs with the usual complex multiplication, written C F), embedding each vector uu on the imaginary axis. A vector squared is then (ui)(ui)=−u2(ui)(ui) = -u^2, which is exactly Q(u)Q(u) — the contraction axiom here is the familiar i2=−1i^2 = -1.

The quadratic form is assembled from two pieces the library already has: square_form, the form u↦uuu \mapsto u\,u on a field, and neg_form, which negates a quadratic form and proves the result is one. Everything else the class asks for is a structure proved earlier in the same file, so the instance is a literal naming them:

  Definition GA_Cliff1 : GeometricAlgebra F F (C F) (Q := neg_form square_form) :=
    {| GeometricAlgebra.ring         := C_Ring;
       GeometricAlgebra.scaling      := Cliff1_ScaleLaws;
       GeometricAlgebra.mul_linear_l := fun y =>
         {| Linear.proper := fun x x' Hx => Magma.op_proper x x' Hx y y (reflexivity y);
            Linear.is_additive := fun x x' => right_distributive x x' y;
            Linear.is_homogeneous := fun a x => cliff1_ga_scale_mul_l a x y |};
       GeometricAlgebra.mul_linear_r := fun x =>
         {| Linear.proper := fun y y' Hy => Magma.op_proper x x (reflexivity x) y y' Hy;
            Linear.is_additive := fun y y' => left_distributive x y y';
            Linear.is_homogeneous := fun a y => cliff1_ga_scale_mul_r a x y |};
       GeometricAlgebra.ga_vec       := cliff1_ga_vec;
       GeometricAlgebra.vec_linear :=
         {| Linear.proper         := cliff1_ga_vec_proper;
            Linear.is_additive    := cliff1_ga_vec_add;
            Linear.is_homogeneous := cliff1_ga_vec_scale |};
       GeometricAlgebra.contraction  := cliff1_ga_contract |}.

Every entry is either a structure proved once (C_Ring, the complex numbers are a ring; Cliff1_ScaleLaws, the module laws for scaling a complex number by a field element) or a three-line Linear literal whose three fields are lemmas from the same file. The carriers are implicit — Coq reads F, V and A off the stated type — and the whole thing is checked, for an arbitrary field, by a one-line example:

Example ga_cliff1_check (F : Type) `{FF : Field F} :
  GeometricAlgebra F F (C F) (Q := neg_form square_form) := GA_Cliff1.

With the instance in scope, polarization — proved above once, for every geometric algebra — is a theorem about complex numbers with no further work.
