Inner products, lengths, and the polarization identity

A vector space lets you add and stretch, and that is all. It has no notion of length, of angle, or of two directions being perpendicular. So: what is the least you must add to get them?

Start from what you want. Length should be recoverable from the operation applied to a vector and itself, ∥v∥2=⟨v,v⟩\|v\|^2 = \langle v,v\rangle. Angle should come from the operation applied to two different vectors — on the blackboard ⟨u,v⟩=∥u∥∥v∥cos⁡θ\langle u,v\rangle = \|u\|\,\|v\|\cos\theta, so perpendicular should mean the operation returns 00. Both readings need the operation to be symmetric, since neither length nor angle distinguishes an ordered pair from its reverse.

That leaves one more demand, and it is the one that makes the thing computable rather than merely defined: it should interact with the vector space operations, so that knowing the operation on a few vectors determines it on every combination of them. That is linearity in an argument.

Symmetry and linearity in one argument. That is the whole list, and this post is what follows from it — including the fact that the last paragraph’s two readings are not independent: the angles are already determined by the lengths.

The structure

An inner product on a vector space VV over a field FF is an operation ⟨⋅,⋅⟩:V×V→F\langle\,\cdot\,,\cdot\,\rangle : V \times V \to F that is linear in its first argument and symmetric:

⟨u+u′,v⟩=⟨u,v⟩+⟨u′,v⟩,⟨a*:u,v⟩=a⟨u,v⟩,⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩.\langle u + u', v\rangle = \langle u, v\rangle + \langle u', v\rangle, \qquad \langle a *: u, v\rangle = a\,\langle u, v\rangle, \qquad \langle u, v\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle.

  Class InnerProductSpace (F V : Type) `{VectorSpace F V} {Vi : HasInner F V} := {
    inner_linear   :: forall v : V, Linear F V F (fun u => ⟪ u , v ⟫);
    is_commutative :: Commutative V F inner;
  }.

Two fields for three laws, because the first two are exactly what Linear says: freeze the second argument and what remains, fun u => ⟪ u , v ⟫, is a linear map from VV to FF. Rather than restate additivity and homogeneity, the class reuses the class the previous post defined. Symmetry is the catalogue’s Commutative, read at an operation whose output type differs from its inputs’.

Notice what is absent: no positivity, no requirement that ⟨u,u⟩≥0\langle u,u\rangle \geq 0. That needs an ordered field to even state, and everything in this post holds without it — over the complex numbers, over finite fields, over anything.

Length gets a name but not a square root, which would not exist in a general field:

Definition norm2 {F V : Type} `{InnerProductSpace F V} (u : V) : F := ⟪ u , u ⟫.

Symmetry does the work twice

The class demands linearity in the first argument only. Linearity in the second is a theorem, and its proof is the pattern for everything below: step across with symmetry, use the law you have, step back.

⟨u,v+w⟩=⟨v+w,u⟩=⟨v,u⟩+⟨w,u⟩=⟨u,v⟩+⟨u,w⟩\langle u, v + w\rangle = \langle v + w, u\rangle = \langle v, u\rangle + \langle w, u\rangle = \langle u, v\rangle + \langle u, w\rangle

  Theorem inner_right_additive : forall u v w : V,
    ⟪ u , v + w ⟫ == ⟪ u , v ⟫ + ⟪ u , w ⟫.
  Proof.
    intros u v w.
    calc (⟪ u , v + w ⟫)
      == (⟪ v + w , u ⟫)              { commutative }
      == (⟪ v , u ⟫ + ⟪ w , u ⟫)      { inner_left_additive }
      == (⟪ u , v ⟫ + ⟪ w , u ⟫)      { (commutative v) }
      == (⟪ u , v ⟫ + ⟪ u , w ⟫)      { (commutative w) }.
  Qed.

The same three-step shape gives inner_right_scale, and the zero and negation facts come from the group post yet again — freezing an argument leaves an additive map, so additive_zero applies and ⟨0,u⟩=0\langle 0, u\rangle = 0 needs no calculation of its own.

Expanding a square

Now the familiar identity. On the blackboard, expanding ⟨u+v,u+v⟩\langle u+v, u+v\rangle by linearity in each argument gives four terms, which regroup into

∥u+v∥2=(∥u∥2+∥v∥2)+(⟨u,v⟩+⟨v,u⟩).\|u+v\|^2 = \big(\|u\|^2 + \|v\|^2\big) + \big(\langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,u\rangle\big).

  Theorem inner_expand : forall u v : V,
    ⟪ u + v , u + v ⟫ == (⟪ u , u ⟫ + ⟪ v , v ⟫) + (⟪ u , v ⟫ + ⟪ v , u ⟫).
  Proof.
    intros u v.
    calc (⟪ u + v , u + v ⟫)
      == (⟪ u , u + v ⟫ + ⟪ v , u + v ⟫)
        { inner_left_additive }
      == ((⟪ u , u ⟫ + ⟪ u , v ⟫) + ⟪ v , u + v ⟫)
        { inner_right_additive }
      == ((⟪ u , u ⟫ + ⟪ u , v ⟫) + (⟪ v , u ⟫ + ⟪ v , v ⟫))
        { inner_right_additive }
      == ((⟪ u , u ⟫ + ⟪ v , v ⟫) + (⟪ u , v ⟫ + ⟪ v , u ⟫))
        { add_rotate }.
  Qed.

Three of the four steps are linearity, one in each argument. The last step is the interesting one: it is add_rotate, one of the rearrangement lemmas from the abelian-group post, doing nothing but moving four summands into the grouping the statement wants. That is the division of labour the whole library is arranged around — the geometry supplies three steps, and the bookkeeping step is a lemma proved once, elsewhere, about any abelian group.

Since ⟨u,v⟩=⟨v,u⟩\langle u,v\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle, the last bracket is twice the inner product, and reading the identity backwards recovers ⟨u,v⟩\langle u,v\rangle from lengths alone. That is the polarization identity: an inner product is determined by the lengths it induces.

The same identity, in a geometric algebra

Geometric algebra takes a different route to the same place. Instead of a scalar-valued product on vectors, it embeds the vectors in an algebra where they can be multiplied and stay in the algebra, subject to one axiom: a vector times itself is the scalar Q(u)Q(u) its quadratic form assigns, times the identity.

    contraction : forall u : V, ga_vec u * ga_vec u == Q u *: 1;

From that single equation, the symmetric part of the product of two different vectors is forced. Squaring u+vu+v and subtracting the squares of uu and vv leaves

uv+vu=(Q(u+v)−Q(u)−Q(v))⋅1,uv + vu = \big(Q(u+v) - Q(u) - Q(v)\big) \cdot 1,

and the bracket — the polar form of QQ, written polar Q u v — is the inner product in disguise.

Nothing in that argument uses vectors. It works for any two ring elements whose squares happen to be scalars, so that is how it is proved: x and y are algebra elements, and “squares to a scalar” is a hypothesis rather than an axiom.

  Theorem scalar_squares_polarization : forall (x y : A) (p q s : F),
    x * x == p *: 1 -> y * y == q *: 1 -> (x + y) * (x + y) == s *: 1 ->
    x * y + y * x == ((s + - p) + - q) *: 1.
  Proof.
    intros x y p q s Hx Hy Hs.
    apply (cancel_l (p *: (1 : A) + q *: 1)).

The proof opens with cancel_l — the group cancellation law from the group post — to add p+qp + q to both sides, after which expanding (x+y)(x+y)(x+y)(x+y) and two distributivity steps finish it. The vector statement is the instance x=ux = u, y=vy = v as elements of the algebra, with the contraction axiom supplying all three hypotheses:

  Theorem polarization : forall u v : V,
    ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u == polar Q u v *: (1 : A).

So the two developments meet: an inner product built from lengths, and a product of vectors whose symmetric part is that inner product. The geometric algebra posts later in the series build on this equation, and Theory/Exterior.v gets its own statement of it for free — Proof. exact polarization. Qed.
