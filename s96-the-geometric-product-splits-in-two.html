The geometric product splits in two, and one half costs you a division

Multiply two vectors in a geometric algebra and you get something that is not a vector. The textbook writes the result as a sum of two pieces,

uv=u⋅v+u∧v,uv \;=\; u\cdot v \;+\; u\wedge v,

a scalar and a bivector: how much the two vectors agree, and the oriented plane they span. This post is about where that split comes from, why the obvious proof produces every term twice, and what the factor of one half actually costs — because in Coq you cannot write “divide by 2” without first proving that 2 has an inverse, and in some fields it does not.

One axiom, not two

A geometric algebra is a ring A containing a vector space V, with a single law tying the two together: a vector times itself is a scalar, namely its own square length.

contraction : forall u : V, ga_vec u * ga_vec u == Q u *: 1;

Nothing here mentions a dot product or a wedge product. Both are consequences. Expand contraction at u + v and the cross terms survive:

(u+v)(u+v)=uu+uv+vu+vv,(u+v)(u+v) = uu + uv + vu + vv,

and since the three squares are scalars, so is uv + vu. That symmetric combination is the dot product, doubled. Its antisymmetric partner uv - vu is the wedge, also doubled. Adding the two recovers 2uv.

So the split falls out of the one axiom — but it falls out doubled. Every term arrives twice, and undoubling means dividing by two.

The doubled form needs no division

Stated with the doubling left in, the decomposition holds over any field whatsoever, and that is how Theory/Exterior.v states it. No inverse of 2 appears, so nothing prevents a field where 1 + 1 == 0.

That matters more than it sounds. Characteristic 2 is not an exotic corner: it is where coding theory and much of computer algebra live. A development that divides by 2 early quietly excludes them all.

The undoubled form needs an ordered field

To get the textbook statement you need half 1, and to get half 1 you need 2 to be invertible. Theory/Order.v supplies exactly that, by an argument that is about order rather than about arithmetic: two = 1 + 1 is a sum of two strictly positive things, so it is strictly positive, so it is not zero, so in a field it has an inverse.

That is why Theory/GAWedgeDot.v asks for an OrderedField where Theory/Exterior.v asks only for a Field. The extra hypothesis buys one thing: the ability to halve. With it, the decomposition takes its familiar shape:

  Theorem ga_product_decompose : forall u v : V,
    ga_vec u * ga_vec v
    == ⟪ u , v ⟫ *: (1 : A) + half 1 *: wedge (ga_vec u) (ga_vec v).

Read it as uv = u·v + u∧v with the wedge carrying its conventional factor of one half. The wedge itself is defined on algebra elements, wedge x y := x * y + - (y * x) for any x y : A — nothing about it needs the arguments to be vectors — so the statement embeds the two vectors and wedges their images. The proof is a calc chain that doubles the product, splits the doubled form into its symmetric and antisymmetric halves, and then halves each:

    calc ((ga_vec u * ga_vec v) : A)
      == ((half 1 *: (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec u * ga_vec v)) : A)
        { sc_half_double }

The first step is the only interesting one: sc_half_double says half 1 *: (x + x) == x, so multiplying by one is really multiplying by half 1 * two, arranged so the doubling that the axiom forces on us can be introduced deliberately instead of apologised for.

The step after it is the split itself, and it is written unusually:

      ==[hard] ((half 1 *: ((ga_vec u * ga_vec v + ga_vec v * ga_vec u)
            + (ga_vec u * ga_vec v + - (ga_vec v * ga_vec u)))) : A)

[hard] names the step and defers its justification to the end of the proof, where it is discharged by a chain of its own. The rearrangement being justified is entirely about the sum inside the scaling: add a zero, write that zero as vu+(−vu)vu + (-vu), then interchange the middle two summands so the symmetric and antisymmetric pairs sit together.

xx=xx+0=xx+(vu+(−vu))=(x+vu)+(x+(−vu))xx \;=\; xx + 0 \;=\; xx + \big(vu + (-vu)\big) \;=\; (x + vu) + \big(x + (-vu)\big)

Written as an ordinary chain, every line would have to carry the half 1 *: ( … ) wrapper along for the ride. The calcrw form proves the inner rewrite on its own and then rewrites it into place, so the wrapper is written once, in the step’s target. Both the naming of a step and this form come from Lib/Calc.v; they exist so that a proof can be shaped like the argument rather than like the term.

Starting from the other end

Textbooks are split on which of the two facts is the definition. The other convention takes the dot and wedge as given — a symmetric form and an alternating bilinear form — and defines the product as their sum. WedgeDotPresentation is that convention written as a class:

  wd_w           : V -> V -> A;
  wd_w_proper    :: Proper (equ ==> equ ==> equ) wd_w;
  wd_w_add_l     : forall u u' v : V, wd_w (u + u') v == wd_w u v + wd_w u' v;

with wd_w the wedge and a field saying the product decomposes as ⟪u,v⟫ *: 1 + wd_w u v.

The class demands wd_w u u == 0 — the form is alternating — rather than the antisymmetry wd_w u v == - wd_w v u, and with division by 2 already under suspicion the choice matters. Alternating implies antisymmetric over any field: expand wd_w (u+v) (u+v) == 0 by bilinearity and the two diagonal terms vanish, which is wd_w_anti in the file. The converse expansion only yields wd_w u u + wd_w u u == 0, and in characteristic 2 that does not force wd_w u u == 0. The alternating law is the one that survives everywhere, so it is the primitive; Alternating in Structure/Bilinear.v makes the same choice for scalar-valued forms, for the same reason.

The two presentations are inter-derivable, and the pair of theorems saying so is the point of the file. But they are not inter-derivable on the same terms, and the asymmetry is worth stating plainly.

Going from wedge-and-dot to contraction is free:

  Theorem wedgedot_to_contract : forall u : V,
    wd_vec u * wd_vec u == norm2 u *: (1 : A).

Set v := u; the wedge of a vector with itself is zero because the form is alternating, so the decomposition collapses to the contraction axiom in three calc steps. No order, no halving, no division: this direction holds over any field, characteristic 2 included.

Going the other way is not free. Building a WedgeDotPresentation out of a contraction-first algebra means producing the wedge, and the wedge you produce is half 1 *: wedge — so this direction wants the halving, and with it the ordered field.

The asymmetry is real rather than an artifact of how the proofs were written. One direction discards information (a wedge, when you set both arguments equal, tells you nothing); the other must manufacture it, and manufacturing it costs a division by two.
