A dual space is a recorded isomorphism with Hom(V, F)

Take a vector space VV over a field FF, and look at the scalar-valued linear maps on it — the functions V→FV \to F that respect addition and scaling. Such a function is called a linear functional. Functionals can themselves be added and scaled, one input at a time, so they form a vector space of their own, called the dual space of VV.

But which space, concretely, is the dual? If VV is the space of column vectors, the row vectors deserve the name: a row times a column is a scalar, and every functional arises that way. So does the space of coordinate functions {1,…,n}→F\{1,\dots,n\} \to F. So, trivially, does the space of linear maps V→FV \to F itself. Informal mathematics leaves the choice open: the dual is defined only up to isomorphism, and any carrier isomorphic to the right one serves. A formalization must say exactly what “DD is a dual of VV” means, in a way that lets every reasonable carrier qualify, makes any two qualifying carriers provably isomorphic, and does not force each theorem about duals to be re-proved per carrier.

Textbooks also equip a dual with a pairing: a two-argument operation ⟨d,v⟩\langle d, v \rangle that applies the functional dd denotes to the vector vv, and they list its properties — linear in each argument separately, and zero for all vv only when dd is zero — as axioms to be checked for each candidate carrier. Are those really axioms, or do they follow from the definition for free?

The definition: one clause

Two earlier results carry the whole construction, so here they are in brief. The linear maps from UU to VV form a vector space Hom(U,V)\mathrm{Hom}(U,V) — add and scale maps pointwise, and every law holds because it holds in VV at each input (the maps-form-a-vector-space post builds it as LinearMap, a function bundled with its linearity proof). And “these two vector spaces are the same up to relabeling” is itself a record, VSIso: a pair of mutually inverse linear maps, with symmetry and transitivity operations to chain such facts (the curry post builds it and shows what it buys over a bare pair of inverse functions).

With those two in hand, “realizes” is one clause, from Structure/Realizes.v:

Class Realizes (F W D : Type)
    {Fe : EqRel F} {Fz : Zero F} {Fa : HasAdd F} {Fn : HasNeg F} {Fo : One F}
    {Fm : HasMult F} {Fi : HasInv F} {FF : Field F}
    {eW : EqRel W} {zW : Zero W} {aW : HasAdd W} {nW : HasNeg W} {sW : HasScale F W}
    {VW : VectorSpace F W}
    {eD : EqRel D} {zD : Zero D} {aD : HasAdd D} {nD : HasNeg D} {sD : HasScale F D}
    {VD : VectorSpace F D} :=
  realization : VSIso F D W.

Hint Mode Realizes - ! ! - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - - : typeclass_instances.

DD realizes WW when a linear isomorphism between them has been chosen and recorded. The long brace-enclosed prefix is only the field and the two vector-space structures; the content is the single clause after :=. Declaring it as a class puts each recorded choice in the database that instance search consults, and the Hint Mode line disciplines that search: the two ! positions are the carriers WW and DD, and they demand that search fire only when both are already concrete types, never unification variables. Search may retrieve a recorded isomorphism; it may never construct one by speculating about carriers. By convention the database holds one recorded choice per carrier pair, so retrieval is unambiguous.

Two general facts make “realizes” behave the way “is a dual of” should. First, the canonical carrier realizes itself, by the identity isomorphism:

  #[export] Instance realizes_self : Realizes F W W := vsiso_refl.

Second, any two realizations of the same WW are isomorphic to each other — go through WW: forward along one recorded isomorphism, backward along the other. That is the whole proof, in Theory/Realizes.v:

  Definition realizes_unique : VSIso F D1 D2 :=
    vsiso_trans R1 (vsiso_sym R2).

A dual space is now a special case, from Structure/DualSpace.v: DD is a dual of VV over FF when DD realizes Hom(V,F)\mathrm{Hom}(V,F).

Class DualSpace (F V D : Type) `{VectorSpace F V}
    {eD : EqRel D} {zD : Zero D} {aD : HasAdd D} {nD : HasNeg D}
    {sD : HasScale F D} {VD : VectorSpace F D} := {
  represent :: Realizes F (LinearMap F V F) D;
}.

The single field DualSpace.represent is the recorded isomorphism between DD and the space of functionals.

The pairing is derived, not assumed

The pairing reads an element of DD as the functional it denotes — follow the recorded isomorphism, then apply. From Theory/DualSpace.v:

Definition dual_apply {F V D : Type} `{DualSpace F V D} (d : D) (v : V) : F :=
  VSIso.to DualSpace.represent d v.

Notation "⟨ d , v ⟩" := (dual_apply d v).

Here VSIso.to DualSpace.represent is the forward half of the isomorphism, a linear map from DD to Hom(V,F)\mathrm{Hom}(V,F). A note on brackets: the library writes ⟪ u , v ⟫ for the inner product of two vectors of the same space (the inner operation, central to the polarization post), matching Lean’s mathlib; the dual pairing keeps the single brackets ⟨ d , v ⟩, and the types keep the two apart — a functional-carrier element on the left, a vector on the right.

Now the textbook properties. A two-argument map is bilinear when it is linear in each argument separately. The pairing is bilinear with no new proof, because it is exactly the uncurrying of a linear map into Hom(V,F)\mathrm{Hom}(V,F) — the curry post showed that uncurry g, the function (d,v)↦gdv(d, v) \mapsto g\,d\,v, is bilinear whenever gg is linear and each gdg\,d is. Both hold here: VSIso.to DualSpace.represent is linear, and each of its values is a LinearMap. So the instance is one application:

  #[export] Instance dual_apply_bilinear : Bilinear F D V F dual_apply :=
    BilinearMap.bilinear (uncurry (VSIso.to DualSpace.represent)).

The individual laws are then projections of the general law classes — Additive and Homogeneous from the linearity-is-two-laws post — found by instance search inside that Bilinear fact. Two of the four, one per argument; the statement first, on the blackboard:

⟨d+d′,v⟩=⟨d,v⟩+⟨d′,v⟩and⟨d,a*:v⟩=a⋅⟨d,v⟩.\langle d + d',\, v \rangle = \langle d, v \rangle + \langle d', v \rangle \qquad\text{and}\qquad \langle d,\, a *: v \rangle = a \cdot \langle d, v \rangle.

  Theorem dual_apply_add_l : forall (d d' : D) (v : V),
    (⟨ d + d' , v ⟩) == (⟨ d , v ⟩) + (⟨ d' , v ⟩).
  Proof.
    intros d d' v. exact (additive[fun x : D => ⟨ x , v ⟩] d d').
  Qed.

  Theorem dual_apply_scale_r : forall (a : F) (d : D) (v : V),
    (⟨ d , a *: v ⟩) == a * (⟨ d , v ⟩).
  Proof.
    intros a d v.
    exact (homogeneous[dual_apply d] a v).
  Qed.

Each proof is a single exact: name the function, and instance search finds the Additive (respectively Homogeneous) fact inside dual_apply_bilinear. The mirror laws dual_apply_add_r and dual_apply_scale_l are the same one-liners on the other argument, and dual_apply_linear / dual_apply_linear_l package each slot’s linearity whole.

The remaining textbook axiom is non-degeneracy: nothing pairs to zero against every vector except zero itself. On the blackboard, writing to\mathrm{to} and from\mathrm{from} for the two halves of the recorded isomorphism and assuming to(d)\mathrm{to}(d) is the zero functional:

d=from(to(d)){round trip}=from(0){to(d)=0}=0{linear maps send 0 to 0}.d \;=\; \mathrm{from}(\mathrm{to}(d)) \quad \{\text{round trip}\} \;=\; \mathrm{from}(0) \quad \{\mathrm{to}(d) = 0\} \;=\; 0 \quad \{\text{linear maps send } 0 \text{ to } 0\}.

In Coq, as a calc chain — the tactic that turns a written chain of equalities into transitivity steps, one justification each, from the calc post:

  Theorem dual_apply_nondegenerate : forall d : D,
    (forall v : V, (⟨ d , v ⟩) == 0) -> d == 0.
  Proof.
    intros d Hz.
    calc d
      == (VSIso.from DualSpace.represent (VSIso.to DualSpace.represent d))
           { vsiso_from_to }
      == (VSIso.from DualSpace.represent 0)
           { (Linear.proper (f := VSIso.from DualSpace.represent) _ 0 Hz) }
      == (0 : D)
           { (additive_zero (VSIso.from DualSpace.represent)) }.
  Qed.

Walking it: vsiso_from_to is the isomorphism’s round-trip law, rewriting dd as from(to(d))\mathrm{from}(\mathrm{to}(d)); Linear.proper says a linear map respects the chosen equality, and the hypothesis Hz says precisely that to(d)\mathrm{to}(d) equals the zero functional pointwise, so the argument can be replaced by 00; and additive_zero — proved once for group homomorphisms back in the groups post — closes the chain, because a linear map is in particular additive and so sends zero to zero. Bilinearity and non-degeneracy, the entire axiom list, are theorems.

The canonical dual, and uniqueness

Hom(V,F)\mathrm{Hom}(V,F) itself is a dual of VV: its one field is realizes_self, and instance search fills it in, so the declaration is empty braces.

  #[export] Instance Hom_DualSpace : DualSpace F V (LinearMap F V F) := {}.

And “the dual is well defined up to isomorphism” is realizes_unique instantiated at W=Hom(V,F)W = \mathrm{Hom}(V,F): any two carriers that are duals of the same VV are isomorphic.

  Definition dual_unique : VSIso F D1 D2 :=
    realizes_unique (W := LinearMap F V F).

A smoke test in the source confirms the canonical instance is found by search alone — the _ asks Coq to fill the whole structure:

Example hom_dualspace_found (F V : Type) `{FF : Field F}
    {eV : EqRel V} {zV : Zero V} {aV : HasAdd V} {nV : HasNeg V}
    {sV : HasScale F V} {VV : VectorSpace F V} :
  DualSpace F V (LinearMap F V F) := _.

Finite dimension: the dual basis

Everything so far held for any VV. When VV is nn-dimensional the dual is too, and the isomorphisms can be written down. Recall from the basis post what a basis is here: a Basis n F V packages nn vectors e1,…,ene_1, \dots, e_n together with a linear coordinate map, such that rebuilding a vector from its coordinates and reading the coordinates of a rebuilt combination are both the identity — lincomb is the rebuilding operation, a sum of scaled basis vectors.

The dual basis consists of the coordinate functionals e1,…,ene^1, \dots, e^n: the functional eie^i sends a vector to its ii-th coordinate. Each is linear because the coordinate map is, coordinate by coordinate. From Theory/DualBasis.v:

  Definition basis_coord (i : I) : LinearMap F V F :=
    linear_map_from_laws (fun v => Basis.coords B v i)
      (fun u v Huv => coords_proper B u v Huv i)
      (fun u v => coords_add B u v i)
      (fun a v => coords_scale B a v i).

For these functionals to form a basis of Hom(V,F)\mathrm{Hom}(V,F), they need a coordinate map of their own, and there is an obvious candidate: the coordinates of a functional ff are its values on the basis vectors, f(e1),…,f(en)f(e_1), \dots, f(e_n) — that is dual_coords, linear in ff because the structure on Hom\mathrm{Hom} is pointwise. The substantial direction of the round trip is reconstruction: every functional is the combination of coordinate functionals weighted by its values on the basis,

f=∑if(ei)ei.f \;=\; \sum_i f(e_i)\, e^i.

Both sides are functionals, so test them at an arbitrary vector vv and chain:

(∑if(ei)ei)(v)=∑if(ei)⋅ei(v)=∑iei(v)⋅f(ei)=f(∑iei(v)ei)=f(v),\Big(\sum_i f(e_i)\, e^i\Big)(v) \;=\; \sum_i f(e_i)\cdot e^i(v) \;=\; \sum_i e^i(v) \cdot f(e_i) \;=\; f\Big(\sum_i e^i(v)\, e_i\Big) \;=\; f(v),

by, in order: evaluation passes through a sum of functionals term by term; multiplication in FF commutes; ff is linear, so a sum of scaled values is ff of the corresponding combination; and the combination ∑iei(v)ei\sum_i e^i(v)\,e_i rebuilds vv from its own coordinates. The Coq proof is that chain, step for step:

  Lemma dual_reconstruction : forall f : LinearMap F V F,
    lincomb basis_coord (dual_coords f) == f.
  Proof.
    intros f v.
    calc (lincomb basis_coord (dual_coords f) v)
      == (bigopI (fun i => f (Basis.vec B i) * Basis.coords B v i))
      == (bigopI (fun i => Basis.coords B v i * f (Basis.vec B i)))
      == (f (lincomb (Basis.vec B) (Basis.coords B v)))
      == (f v).
    - exact (hom_bigopI_at (fun i => f (Basis.vec B i) *: basis_coord i) v).
    - apply bigopI_ext. intros i.
      exact (commutative (f (Basis.vec B i)) (Basis.coords B v i)).
    - symmetry.
      exact (lm_lincomb f (Basis.vec B) (Basis.coords B v)).
    - apply (Linear.proper (f := LinearMap.map f)).
      exact (Basis.reconstruction B v).
  Qed.

Walking it: intros f v fixes the functional and the test vector — equality of functionals is pointwise, so one arbitrary point suffices. bigopI is the finite sum over the index carrier’s enumeration; hom_bigopI_at is the first blackboard step, pushing evaluation at v through the sum; bigopI_ext rewrites under the sum term by term with commutative, the second step; lm_lincomb is ff’s linearity applied to a whole combination, the third; and Basis.reconstruction, transported through f by Linear.proper, is the fourth. The other round trip, dual_coords_lincomb, reads the weights back off a combination of coordinate functionals by evaluating at basis vectors — the value of eie^i at eje_j is the Kronecker delta δij\delta_{ij}, and the deltas collapse the sum. With both round trips proved, the dual basis is assembled field by field:

  Definition dual_basis : Basis n I F (LinearMap F V F) :=
    {| Basis.vec := basis_coord;
       Basis.coords := dual_coords;
       Basis.reconstruction := dual_reconstruction;
       Basis.coords_linear_combination := dual_coords_lincomb |}.

So the canonical dual of an nn-dimensional space is nn-dimensional. And any other dual carrier DD inherits the count: transport the dual basis along DD’s recorded isomorphism, using basis_transport from Theory/Transport.v (map each basis vector across, conjugate the coordinate map):

  Definition dual_space_basis : Basis n I F D :=
    basis_transport (VSIso.from DualSpace.represent) (VSIso.to DualSpace.represent)
      (vsiso_to_from DualSpace.represent) (vsiso_from_to DualSpace.represent)
      (dual_basis B).

The double dual, and V as a dual of its dual

Iterate the construction: the dual of the dual. There is a canonical map from VV into it that mentions no basis at all — evaluation, sending a vector vv to the functional-on-functionals f↦f(v)f \mapsto f(v). That map, into_double_dual, lives in Theory/Hom.v and is linear for any VV whatsoever; but without finiteness it need not be onto — an infinite-dimensional space embeds in its double dual without filling it.

A basis supplies the inverse. A double-dual element pp reports, for each coordinate functional eie^i, a scalar p(ei)p(e^i) — treat those scalars as coordinates and rebuild the vector:

  Definition from_double_dual : LinearMap F DDualV V :=
    linear_map_from_laws
      (fun p : DDualV => lincomb (Basis.vec B) (fun i => p (basis_coord i)))
      from_dd_proper from_dd_add from_dd_scale.

(DDualV locally abbreviates the double dual’s canonical carrier, LinearMap F (LinearMap F V F) F.) One round trip is exactly basis reconstruction — evaluate vv at each coordinate functional and you have its coordinates, so rebuilding returns vv:

  Lemma double_dual_fromto : forall v : V,
    from_double_dual (into_double_dual v) == v.
  Proof.
    intros v.
    exact (Basis.reconstruction B v).
  Qed.

The other, double_dual_tofrom, is a calc chain in the same style as dual_reconstruction — push evaluation through the sum, commute, gather with pp’s linearity, and finish with dual_reconstruction itself. Together the two round trips make evaluation an isomorphism in finite dimension:

  Definition double_dual_iso : VSIso F V DDualV :=
    vsiso_from_laws into_double_dual from_double_dual
      double_dual_fromto double_dual_tofrom.

“VV is isomorphic to its double dual” can now be said in the classes: VV is itself a dual space of DD, for any dual carrier DD of VV. The recorded isomorphism from VV to Hom(D,F)\mathrm{Hom}(D,F) is evaluation into the canonical double dual, followed by the contravariant Hom(−,F)\mathrm{Hom}(-,F) step vsiso_hom_dom, which carries DD’s own recorded isomorphism across by precomposition:

  Definition double_dual : DualSpace F D V := {|
    DualSpace.represent :=
      vsiso_trans (double_dual_iso B)
        (vsiso_hom_dom (DualSpace.represent (DualSpace := DS)));
  |}.

And the abstract double-dual theorem is now one line, with no new proof at all. Let DD be any dual of VV and DDDD any dual of DD. Then VV and DDDD are both duals of DD — the first by double_dual, the second by assumption — and duals are unique up to isomorphism:

  Definition double_dual_abstract : VSIso F V DD :=
    dual_unique double_dual DDS.

Using it

The whole apparatus runs on instance search. Choosing the canonical carrier at both layers — Hom(V,F)\mathrm{Hom}(V,F) as the dual, Hom(Hom(V,F),F)\mathrm{Hom}(\mathrm{Hom}(V,F),F) as the double dual — the abstract isomorphism specializes to the concrete evaluation isomorphism’s type, with Hom_DualSpace supplying both dual-space facts:

Example double_dual_abstract_found (F V : Type) `{FF : Field F}
    {eV : EqRel V} {zV : Zero V} {aV : HasAdd V} {nV : HasNeg V}
    {sV : HasScale F V} {VV : VectorSpace F V}
    {n : nat} {I : Type} {Ieq : EqDec I} {IFin : FinIndex n I}
    (B : Basis n I F V) :
  VSIso F V (LinearMap F (LinearMap F V F) F) :=
  double_dual_abstract B Hom_DualSpace Hom_DualSpace.
