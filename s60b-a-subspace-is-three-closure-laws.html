A subspace is three closure laws, and its carrier re-proves nothing

A plane through the origin, sitting inside three-dimensional space, is itself a vector space: add two of its vectors and you stay in the plane, scale one and you stay in the plane. That is the whole phenomenon — a subspace is a subset that the operations cannot escape. Two questions for a formalization. First, what exactly must be assumed about the subset — in particular, must closure under negation be a separate assumption? Second, and less obvious: the elements of the sub-thing are now pairs, a vector together with a proof that it lies in the subset — when are two such pairs equal? If the answer involves comparing the proofs, every theorem drowns; the structure must be set up so that proofs never need comparing.

The predicate

Three closure laws, from Structure/Subspace.v — membership is a predicate P : V -> Prop, and the class packages closure of P under the ambient operations:

  Class SubspacePred (F V : Type) `{VectorSpace F V} (P : V -> Prop) := {
    zero         : P 0;
    add_closed   : forall u v : V, P u -> P v -> P (u + v);
    scale_closed : forall (a : F) (u : V), P u -> P (a *: u);
  }.

0∈P,u,v∈P⇒u+v∈P,u∈P⇒au∈P.0 \in P, \qquad u, v \in P \Rightarrow u + v \in P, \qquad u \in P \Rightarrow a\,u \in P.

Closure under negation is deliberately absent: over a field, −u=(−1)u-u = (-1)\,u, so scale_closed at a=−1a = -1 already provides it. One assumption fewer to discharge every time a subspace is constructed.

The carrier

The sub-carrier is Coq’s dependent pair type: an element of V together with a proof it satisfies P. From Theory/Subspace.v:

Definition Sub (V : Type) (P : V -> Prop) := { v : V | P v }.

Now the equality question. Two pairs could be compared structurally — same vector and same proof — but proofs of P v need not be syntactically equal, and demanding they be would import proof-comparison into every lemma. The library’s setoid discipline (the calc post introduces it: each carrier chooses its own equality ==) dissolves the problem — choose the equality that ignores the proof:

Instance Sub_EqRel {V : Type} `{EqRel V} (P : V -> Prop) :
  EqRel (Sub V P) :=
  fun x y => proj1_sig x == proj1_sig y.

Two subspace elements are equal when their underlying vectors are. The proof component participates in forming elements and never in comparing them.

The operations act on the vector and carry the closure proof along; addition pairs add_closed with the sum, scaling pairs scale_closed. Negation is where the missing closure law would have been needed, and the (−1)(-1) trick lands here:

Instance Sub_Neg {F V : Type} `{VectorSpace F V}
    (P : V -> Prop) {SPP : SubspacePred F V P} : HasNeg (Sub V P) :=
  fun x =>
    exist P ((- (1 : F)) *: proj1_sig x)
      (SubspacePred.scale_closed (- (1 : F)) (proj1_sig x) (proj2_sig x)).

The representative is (−1)u(-1)\,u rather than −u-u — the two are ==-equal in V, but transporting the proof P u across that equality would require P itself to respect ==, an assumption the class does not make. Choosing the representative that scale_closed directly certifies avoids ever needing it.

Every law is inherited

Because == on Sub V P is == on V applied to the projections, every algebraic law transfers by projection — the proof of associativity is the ambient associativity, verbatim:

  Lemma sub_associative : forall x y z : Sub V P, (x + y) + z == x + (y + z).
  Proof.
    intros x y z.
    exact (associative (proj1_sig x) (proj1_sig y) (proj1_sig z)).
  Qed.

The one law with content is the inverse law, since negation used a nonstandard representative. On the blackboard, in VV:

(−1)u+u=−u+u=0,(-1)\,u + u \;=\; -u + u \;=\; 0,

first the vector-space fact (−1)u=−u(-1)\,u = -u (neg1_scale, proved once for every vector space), then the group inverse law. In Coq:

  Lemma sub_left_inverse : forall x : Sub V P, (- x) + x == 0.
  Proof.
    intros x.
    change ((- (1 : F)) *: proj1_sig x + proj1_sig x == 0).
    rewrite (neg1_scale (proj1_sig x)).
    exact (left_inverse _).
  Qed.

The change step is definitional: it restates the Sub-level goal as the V-level fact it already is, and the two blackboard steps finish. Group, abelian group, scaling laws and finally the full structure assemble from these inherited pieces, and the file’s smoke test asks instance search to do the assembly unaided:

Example subspace_vs_check (F V : Type) (P : V -> Prop)
  `{FF : Field F} `{VV : VectorSpace F V} (SP : SubspacePred F V P) :
  VectorSpace F (Sub V P) := _.

The inclusion is linear

A subspace comes with a map back into the ambient space — forget the proof. It is linear, trivially but usefully, packaged as a LinearMap (the bundled maps of the maps-form-a-vector-space post):

  Definition sub_inject : LinearMap F (Sub V P) V :=
    linear_map_from_laws
      (fun x => proj1_sig x)
      sub_inject_proper
      sub_inject_add
      sub_inject_scale.

with all three law obligations closed by reflexivity, since the operations on Sub compute on projections. This is the structure the kernels-and-images post runs on: the kernel and image of a linear map are two SubspacePreds, and everything proved here — the vector-space structure, the linear inclusion — is theirs by instantiation, with zero further proof.
