A subsequence keeps the order; a prefix keeps an unbroken front

Two lists, one derived from the other by discarding some elements: the survivors appear in their original order, none is invented, none is repeated, but there may be gaps anywhere. That relation between lists is the subsequence. Tighten it so that all the discarding happens at the tail — the survivors are an unbroken front — and it becomes the prefix. The pair of them is the vocabulary in which “nothing arrived that was not sent” and “everything arrived up to some point” are said precisely, and the communication-protocol theorems later in this series conclude with exactly these two relations, one apiece. This post develops them on their own, as plain list theory: definitions, the closure laws, and the two proofs with actual content — transitivity, and the fact that a subsequence of full length is the whole list.

Two definitions

From Theory/Subseq.v — generic in the element type, with the arguments in the order (sub, super). The subsequence relation is inductive, three ways to build a valid pair:

  Inductive subseq : list A -> list A -> Prop :=
    | subseq_nil : subseq [] []
    | subseq_keep : forall x r s, subseq r s -> subseq (x :: r) (x :: s)
    | subseq_drop : forall x r s, subseq r s -> subseq r (x :: s).

Read the constructors as a walk along the longer list s, deciding element by element: subseq_keep retains the next element (it must then appear, identically, at the head of the shorter list), subseq_drop discards it, and subseq_nil says a completed walk matches empty against empty. There is no constructor that swaps, edits, or repeats — which is the entire content of the relation. subseq r s is pronounced “r is a subsequence of s”, written r≼sr \preceq s below.

The prefix relation needs no induction at all — “the longer list is the shorter one with something appended”:

  Definition prefix (p s : list A) : Prop := exists s2, s = p ++ s2.

Both relations are reflexive, each by the simplest possible argument: subseq_refl keeps every element, prefix_refl appends nothing. The empty list is below everything in both (subseq_nil_l drops every element; prefix_nil_l appends the whole list).

Transitivity

A subsequence of a subsequence is a subsequence:

u≼v∧v≼w⇒u≼w.u \preceq v \;\wedge\; v \preceq w \;\Longrightarrow\; u \preceq w.

The blackboard argument walks the outer derivation, the one relating vv to ww, keeping uu flexible. Look at the last decision that derivation made about the head of ww:

  • ww ended by dropping its head xx: then vv ignores xx, so uu does too — drop xx and continue with the rest of ww.
  • ww ended by keeping xx: then v=x::v′v = x :: v', and the question becomes how uu relates to x::v′x :: v'. Inverting that derivation gives two cases: either u=x::u′u = x :: u' with u′≼v′u' \preceq v' — keep xx on both sides and continue — or uu skipped the xx, with u≼v′u \preceq v' directly — then drop xx from ww and continue.

The inversion step is packaged as its own lemma, because raw inversion on an inductive relation produces the two cases in an awkward shape:

  Lemma subseq_cons_r_inv : forall r x s,
    subseq r (x :: s) ->
    (exists r', r = x :: r' /\ subseq r' s) \/ subseq r s.

And the transitivity proof is the case analysis above, verbatim:

  Lemma subseq_trans : forall u v w,
    subseq u v -> subseq v w -> subseq u w.
  Proof.
    intros u v w Huv Hvw.
    induction Hvw as [| x v' w' Hvw IH | x v' w' Hvw IH] in u, Huv |- *.
    - exact Huv.
    - destruct (subseq_cons_r_inv u x v' Huv) as [[u' [Hequ Hu']] | Hu].
      + rewrite Hequ. apply subseq_keep. apply IH. exact Hu'.
      + apply subseq_drop. apply IH. exact Hu.
    - apply subseq_drop. apply IH. exact Huv.
  Qed.

One Coq detail carries the proof: the in u, Huv |- * clause generalizes the induction over u and its derivation, so the induction hypothesis IH may be applied to a different, shorter u' in the keep case — exactly what the blackboard’s “continue with u′u'” needs. The three bullets are the three cases: base (v = w = [], so Huv is already the goal), keep (the subseq_cons_r_inv split, each branch re-entering IH), and drop (subseq_drop, then IH with u unchanged).

Full length forces equality

The length of a subsequence never exceeds its parent — every step of the derivation adds one to the parent’s side and at most one to the other (subseq_length, a three-case induction). The sharper fact: if the lengths are equal, no drop ever happened, and the two lists are identical,

r≼s∧|r|=|s|⇒r=s.r \preceq s \;\wedge\; |r| = |s| \;\Longrightarrow\; r = s.

  Lemma subseq_length_eq : forall r s, subseq r s -> length r = length s -> r = s.
  Proof.
    intros r s H.
    induction H as [| x r s H IH | x r s H IH]; intros Hlen.
    - reflexivity.
    - simpl in Hlen.
      assert (Hlen' : length r = length s) by lia.
      f_equal. apply IH. exact Hlen'.
    - exfalso.
      assert (Hle : length r <= length s) by (apply subseq_length; exact H).
      simpl in Hlen. lia.
  Qed.

Induction on the derivation. The keep case peels one shared head (f_equal) and passes equal lengths down. The drop case is the point of the lemma: the derivation claims subseq r (x :: s) by dropping, so length r <= length s by subseq_length — but the hypothesis says length r = S (length s), a contradiction lia refuses. A subsequence has full length only when the walk never dropped, and a walk that never drops copies the list.

Read as a specification, this is a useful degenerate check: a guarantee of the form “the output is a subsequence of the input” collapses to “the output is the input” the moment a length count comes out even. Nothing needs to be re-examined element by element.

A prefix is a subsequence

The tighter relation implies the looser one — keep the whole front, then drop the whole tail:

  Lemma prefix_subseq : forall p s, prefix p s -> subseq p s.
  Proof.
    intros p s [s2 Heq]. subst s.
    induction p as [| x p' IH]; simpl.
    - apply subseq_nil_l.
    - apply subseq_keep. exact IH.
  Qed.

Destructing the prefix hypothesis exposes s as p ++ s2; then induction on p emits one subseq_keep per front element, and subseq_nil_l drops the appended s2 wholesale.

Where the two relations do their work

They are the conclusions of the protocol track. The alternating-bit safety theorem of the ABP post concludes subseq (deliver b0 wire) ms — over a channel that drops and duplicates arbitrarily, what a receiver hands the application is a subsequence of what was sent, and by this post’s reading that single claim rules out corruption, duplication, and reordering at once. The Go-Back-N theorem of the sequence-number post concludes the stronger prefix (gbn_deliver 0 wire) ms, and prefix_subseq is precisely how its subsequence guarantee follows as a corollary. The gap between the two relations — a subsequence that is not a prefix has a hole — is a real protocol distinction with a concrete counterexample, the subject of the acknowledgement post.
