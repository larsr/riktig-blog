Cosine and sine are six laws over one exponential

There are two standard ways to say what cos\cos and sin\sin are, and both of them are analysis. One defines them by their power series and recovers the addition formula by rearranging a product of two infinite sums — which needs a theorem about when that rearrangement is allowed. The other defines them by the unit circle, with tt the arc length from (1,0)(1,0) — which needs arc length, which is an integral.

The identity that gets used, though, is algebraic:

cos⁡(t+u)=cos⁡tcos⁡u−sin⁡tsin⁡u,sin⁡(t+u)=cos⁡tsin⁡u+sin⁡tcos⁡u.\cos(t+u) = \cos t \cos u - \sin t \sin u, \qquad \sin(t+u) = \cos t \sin u + \sin t \cos u.

So: which equations pin the pair down, what follows from those equations and nothing else, and which part genuinely needs limits? This post answers all three. The equations turn out to be six, the consequences include angle addition and the polar form of a product of vectors, and the analysis is confined to one obligation that can be named exactly.

Where the square root of −1-1 comes from

The exponential in the laws below takes an algebra argument, so the equations need an algebra containing an element whose square is −1-1. That element does not have to be postulated. Take the geometric algebra of the plane: it is spanned by the unit 11, two orthogonal vectors e1,e2e_1, e_2, and their product e12=e1e2e_{12} = e_1 e_2, with the vectors squaring to 11 and anticommuting. Then

e12e12=e1e2e1e2=−e1e1e2e2=−1.e_{12} e_{12} = e_1 e_2 e_1 e_2 = -\,e_1 e_1 e_2 e_2 = -1 .

The even part — the span of 11 and e12e_{12}, the elements supported on blades with an even number of generators — is closed under the product, and on it the product is commutative. Impl/CliffordComplex.v takes the even part as a subalgebra of the plane algebra Cl F 2 over any field F, using the predicate cl_even:

Notation Even2 F := (Sub (Cl F 2) cl_even).

Every even element is rotor a b, the combination a+be12a + b\,e_{12} (even_is_rotor), and the two-term product law rotor_mul is the complex multiplication table. The square of the bivector is checked directly:

  Definition e12 : Even2 F := exist cl_even (rotor 0 1) (rotor_is_even 0 1).

  Example e12_squared_in_even : e12 * e12 == - (1).

An inverse for a+be12a + b\,e_{12} is its conjugate over the sum of two squares, (a−be12)/(a2+b2)(a - b\,e_{12})/(a^2+b^2), so the even part is a field exactly when that divisor is nonzero for every nonzero element. That condition has a name of its own, in Structure/MinusOneNotASquare.v:

  Class MinusOneNotASquare (F : Type) `{Field F} :=
    two_squares_vanish : forall a b : F, a * a + b * b == 0 -> a == 0.

The name and the equation say the same thing. If −1=c2-1 = c^2 then c2+12=0c^2 + 1^2 = 0 with the second term nonzero; conversely if a2+b2=0a^2 + b^2 = 0 with b≠0b \neq 0 then (a/b)2=−1(a/b)^2 = -1. It is the condition under which the pair construction F[i]F[i] is a field, and rotor_norm2_nonzero is where the even part uses it —

  Lemma rotor_norm2_nonzero : forall a b : F,
    rotor a b =/= (0 : Cl F 2) -> a * a + b * b =/= 0.

— after which the field instance is assembled from the ring laws already proved and the inverse just built:

  #[export] Instance Even_Field : Field (Even2 F) :=
    { Field.nontrivial := even_nontrivial }.

The other way to build the same field is from coordinate pairs (a,b)(a,b) with the multiplication (a,b)(c,d)=(ac−bd,ad+bc)(a,b)(c,d) = (ac-bd, ad+bc), and the two constructions are not competitors: even_to_C carries the unit, both operations and the inverse to those pairs, C_to_even inverts it (even_C_inverse), and even_to_C_inv is the step that makes it an isomorphism of fields rather than of rings. The record of pairs is the coordinate presentation of the even plane.

The laws

Three operations, each its own class so that a type can carry one without the others — an exponential on the algebra, a cosine and a sine on the scalars (Structure/Circular.v):

Class HasCexp A := cexp : A -> A.
Class HasCos F := cos : F -> F.
Class HasSin F := sin : F -> F.

cexp is the circular exponential. Its argument is an algebra element rather than a scalar, because Euler’s identity is a statement about exp⁡(te)\exp(t\,e) where ee is a square root of −1-1 living in the algebra.

Now the equations. Write t⋅xt \cdot x for scaling an algebra element by a scalar (Coq spells it t *: x). The exponential carries sums of commuting elements to products; the two scalar functions have unit square sum; Euler’s identity holds at every square root of −1-1; the cosine is even and the sine is odd; and the sine is not identically zero:

cexp(x+y)=cexp(x)cexp(y)(xy=yx),cos⁡2t+sin⁡2t=1,\mathrm{cexp}(x+y) = \mathrm{cexp}(x)\,\mathrm{cexp}(y) \quad (xy = yx), \qquad \cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1,

cexp(t⋅e)=(cos⁡t)⋅1+(sin⁡t)⋅e(e2=−1),\mathrm{cexp}(t\cdot e) = (\cos t)\cdot 1 + (\sin t)\cdot e \quad (e^2 = -1),

cos⁡(−t)=cos⁡t,sin⁡(−t)=−sin⁡t,∃t.sin⁡t≠0.\cos(-t) = \cos t, \qquad \sin(-t) = -\sin t, \qquad \exists\, t.\ \sin t \neq 0 .

That is the whole class:

  Class Circular (F A : Type) `{Field F}
      {Ae : EqRel A} {Az : Zero A} {Aa : HasAdd A} {An : HasNeg A}
      {Ao : One A} {Am : HasMult A} {As : HasScale F A}
      {Ax : HasCexp A} {Fc : HasCos F} {Fs : HasSin F} := {
    ring    :: Ring A;
    scaling :: ScaleLaws F A;

    (* Scalars are central: without these the guard on [cexp_add] is
       unusable, since nothing would show that [t *: e] and [s *: e]
       commute.  Same two fields, same spelling, as
       Structure/GeometricAlgebra.v. *)
    mul_linear_l :: forall y : A, Linear F A A (fun x => x * y);
    mul_linear_r :: forall x : A, Linear F A A (fun y => x * y);

    cexp_proper :: Proper (equ ==> equ) Ax;
    cos_proper  :: Proper (equ ==> equ) Fc;
    sin_proper  :: Proper (equ ==> equ) Fs;

    cexp_add : forall x y : A,
      x * y == y * x -> cexp (x + y) == cexp x * cexp y;
    pythagoras : forall t : F, cos t * cos t + sin t * sin t == 1;
    euler : forall (t : F) (e : A),
      e * e == - (1) -> cexp (t *: e) == cos t *: 1 + sin t *: e;
    cos_even : forall t : F, cos (- t) == cos t;
    sin_odd  : forall t : F, sin (- t) == - sin t;

    (* Without this field the constant assignment [cexp _ := 1],
       [cos _ := 1], [sin _ := 0] satisfies every law above
       (Impl/CircleModel.v, the `constant_` lemmas). *)
    sin_nontrivial : exists t : F, sin t =/= 0;
  }.

Three fields are bookkeeping rather than trigonometry. The :: fields ring and scaling say the carrier is a ring and a scalar action satisfying the usual laws; the three Proper fields say each operation respects the carrier’s equality, which is what lets a rewrite step replace equals by equals. The two Linear fields say scalars are central — (a⋅x)y=a⋅(xy)(a\cdot x)y = a\cdot(xy) and x(a⋅y)=a⋅(xy)x(a\cdot y) = a\cdot(xy) — and they earn their place: without them nothing shows that t⋅et\cdot e and u⋅eu \cdot e commute, and the guard on cexp_add could never be discharged on the very line Euler’s identity lives on.

What five of the six laws leave open

The interesting field is the last one. Drop sin_nontrivial and the remaining five equations are satisfied by the constant assignment cexp≡1\mathrm{cexp} \equiv 1, cos⁡≡1\cos \equiv 1, sin⁡≡0\sin \equiv 0 — over every algebra. Impl/CircleModel.v proves that, one law at a time:

  Definition constant_cexp : A -> A := fun _ => 1.
  Definition constant_cos : F -> F := fun _ => 1.
  Definition constant_sin : F -> F := fun _ => 0.

Pythagoras, for instance, becomes 1⋅1+0⋅0=11 \cdot 1 + 0 \cdot 0 = 1, which is a ring identity:

  Lemma constant_pythagoras : forall t : F,
    constant_cos t * constant_cos t + constant_sin t * constant_sin t == 1.
  Proof. intros t. unfold constant_cos, constant_sin. ring. Qed.

and Euler’s identity becomes 1=1⋅1+0⋅e1 = 1\cdot 1 + 0 \cdot e, three steps of scaling laws. The assignment fails exactly one thing, and the file says which:

  Lemma constant_sin_vanishes : forall t : F, constant_sin t == 0.

So an exclusion is needed, and sin_nontrivial is the weakest one: some tt has sin⁡t≠0\sin t \neq 0. Stronger ones rule out too much. “sin\sin is injective” and “cos\cos and sin\sin are jointly injective” would each exclude the constant assignment as well, and each is false of real trigonometry, by periodicity: sin⁡0=sin⁡2π\sin 0 = \sin 2\pi.

The exclusion has a price, and the price is characteristic two

Impl/CircleModel.v then has to exhibit a model where sin\sin is not zero — otherwise the six laws together might be unsatisfiable, and every theorem below would be vacuous. The model in the file lives in characteristic two, and the reason is worth stating, though it is reasoning about the class rather than a theorem inside it.

Take any model. By pythagoras the pair (cos⁡t,sin⁡t)(\cos t, \sin t) satisfies c2+s2=1c^2 + s^2 = 1, and the pairs satisfying that equation form a group under the plane’s multiplication — the circle group. By euler together with cexp_add, t↦(cos⁡t,sin⁡t)t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t) is a group homomorphism from (F,+)(F,+) into it. In characteristic zero (F,+)(F,+) is divisible — every element is nn times another, for every nn — so its image is a divisible subgroup of the circle group. Over ℚ\mathbb{Q}, or any finitely generated field of characteristic zero, that group has no nontrivial divisible subgroup at all, and the homomorphism collapses to the constant one. A non-degenerate characteristic-zero model is therefore an exponential field, and building one is analysis. In characteristic pp every element of (F,+)(F,+) has order pp, so the image is pp-torsion — and the circle group over a field of characteristic pp has no pp-torsion unless p=2p = 2.

In characteristic two the circle degenerates. There −1=1-1 = 1 and squaring is additive, so c2+s2=1c^2 + s^2 = 1 says (1+c+s)2=0(1 + c + s)^2 = 0, hence s=1+cs = 1 + c: the circle is a line, and the parameterization is the identity on one coordinate.

  Lemma unit_sum_line : forall a b : F, a * a + b * b == 1 -> b == 1 + a.

The three operations are then written down with no analysis anywhere. The algebra is the pair algebra C F, which in characteristic two is no longer a field (there −1-1 is a square) but is still an F-algebra:

  Definition circle_cos (t : F) : F := 1 + t.
  Definition circle_sin (t : F) : F := t.
  Definition circle_cexp (z : C F) : C F := mkC (1 + im z) (im z).

Each of the six laws is then a short calculation in characteristic-two arithmetic — circle_pythagoras uses that squaring is additive and that doubling vanishes, circle_cexp_add is the two coordinate identities rotation_re and rotation_im, and circle_sin_nontrivial is exists 1, which is where the two-element field’s own nontriviality enters. The instance is assembled field by field as circular_characteristic_two, and Impl/TwoElementField.v supplies the concrete field it stands on:

Instance Bool_Circular : Circular bool (C bool) :=
  circular_characteristic_two bool_characteristic_two.

which is a model with a sine that moves, by computation:

Example bool_sin_moves : sin true = true.
Proof. reflexivity. Qed.

Angle addition is one equation

Now the consequences, from the six laws alone. Two scalings of one element commute (collinear_commute, three uses of centrality), which discharges the guard on cexp_add along a line and gives cexp_collinear_add: cexp((t+u)⋅x)=cexp(t⋅x)cexp(u⋅x)\mathrm{cexp}((t+u)\cdot x) = \mathrm{cexp}(t \cdot x)\,\mathrm{cexp}(u\cdot x). Expanding each factor by euler and multiplying out with the multiplication table of the plane spanned by 11 and ee (two_term_product, which is the complex table again, derived from e2=−1e^2 = -1 and nothing else — and derived one layer down, in Theory/Algebra.v, over the ring and scaling laws that a Circular algebra and a geometric algebra both carry) gives angle addition. On the blackboard:

cexp((t+u)⋅e)=cexp(t⋅e)cexp(u⋅e){collinear addition}\mathrm{cexp}((t+u)\cdot e) \;=\; \mathrm{cexp}(t\cdot e)\,\mathrm{cexp}(u\cdot e) \quad \{\text{collinear addition}\}

=(cos⁡tcos⁡u−sin⁡tsin⁡u)⋅1+(cos⁡tsin⁡u+sin⁡tcos⁡u)⋅e{euler on each factor, then the table}\;=\; (\cos t \cos u - \sin t \sin u)\cdot 1 \;+\; (\cos t \sin u + \sin t \cos u)\cdot e \quad \{\text{euler on each factor, then the table}\}

and in Theory/Circular.v, as a two-step calc chain — the tactic that turns a written chain of equalities into transitivity steps, explained in the calc post:

  Theorem cexp_angle_addition : forall (t u : F) (e : A),
    e * e == - (1) ->
    cexp ((t + u) *: e)
    == (cos t * cos u + - (sin t * sin u)) *: 1
       + (cos t * sin u + sin t * cos u) *: e.
  Proof.
    intros t u e Hroot.
    calc (cexp ((t + u) *: e))
      == ((cexp (t *: e) * cexp (u *: e)) : A)
         { cexp_collinear_add }
      == (((cos t * cos u + - (sin t * sin u)) *: 1
             + (cos t * sin u + sin t * cos u) *: e) : A)
         { (euler_product t u e Hroot) }.
  Qed.

Both steps are named lemmas: the first is collinear addition, the second is euler_product, which is euler applied to each factor followed by two_term_product. That is angle addition in full — as one equation in the algebra.

Splitting it into the two scalar identities the tables print takes something the class does not have. All six laws hold in the one-element algebra, where every element is 00 and every equation is true, so nothing in them makes the coefficients of 11 and ee readable off an element of their span. The missing statement is named and taken as a hypothesis:

Definition independent_from_one (F : Type) {A : Type}
    {Fe : EqRel F} {Fz : Zero F}
    {Ae : EqRel A} {Az : Zero A} {Aa : HasAdd A} {Ao : One A}
    {As : HasScale F A} (e : A) : Prop :=
  forall a b : F, a *: 1 + b *: e == 0 -> a == 0 /\ b == 0.

independent_separates turns that into uniqueness of coordinates, and the two familiar identities follow by comparing the two sides of euler_angle_addition coordinate by coordinate:

  Theorem cos_angle_addition : forall t u : F,
    cos (t + u) == cos t * cos u + - (sin t * sin u).

The hypothesis is a real one, discharged where it holds: plane_independent_from_one proves it for the plane algebra’s own bivector rotor 0 1 (a square root of −1-1 by plane_bivector_square), reading off the two blades, and bool_circle_i_independent proves it in the characteristic-two model, where scale_one_scale_i says a⋅1+b⋅ia\cdot 1 + b \cdot i is literally the pair mkC a b. In that model the separated identities are then examples that hold of an actual pair of functions (bool_cos_angle_addition, bool_sin_angle_addition), not of a degenerate one.

Two further consequences, both from the same six laws. Composition of rotors is addition of angles — rotor_angle_addition says R(t)R(u)=R(t+u)R(t)R(u) = R(t+u) for R(t)=cos⁡t+(sin⁡t)eR(t) = \cos t + (\sin t)e, where ee is any square root of −1-1 in the algebra, with rotor_angle_unit (a rotor at an angle has unit norm) being exactly pythagoras and rotor_angle_reverse (reversal negates the angle) being exactly the two parity laws — that last one asking, in addition, for a reversal that negates ee. And the polar form: for two vectors of the plane,

uv=∥u∥∥v∥cexp(t⋅e),u\,v \;=\; \|u\|\,\|v\|\;\mathrm{cexp}(t\cdot e),

where cos⁡t\cos t and sin⁡t\sin t are identified as the grade-0 and grade-2 parts of the product divided by ∥u∥∥v∥\|u\|\|v\| — the projections of the grading post:

  Theorem polar_product_normalized : forall (u v e : A) (t : F),
    e * e == - (1) ->
    ‖ u ‖ * ‖ v ‖ =/= 0 ->
    ⟨ u * v ⟩_ 1 == 0 ->
    inv (‖ u ‖ * ‖ v ‖) *: ⟨ u * v ⟩_ 0 == cos t *: 1 ->
    inv (‖ u ‖ * ‖ v ‖) *: ⟨ u * v ⟩_ 2 == sin t *: e ->
    u * v == (‖ u ‖ * ‖ v ‖) *: cexp (t *: e).

No inverse cosine appears, and no completeness of the reals: the angle enters as a hypothesis about the two grade parts, and the theorem is the equation those parts satisfy. polar_modulus checks the identification against Pythagoras — the two coefficients of the polar form have square sum (∥u∥∥v∥)2(\|u\|\|v\|)^2.

Summation as equations

The characteristic-zero model is the series one, and it needs a sum. Summation enters the same way every other operation has: as a class with laws, Structure/Series.v. ssum f is the sum of a family f:ℕ→Af : \mathbb{N} \to A and summable f is the domain on which the laws hold.

Each law is guarded by summable, and the guard is not bureaucracy. Consider a total ssum obeying an unguarded parity law, ∑f=∑kf(2k)+∑kf(2k+1)\sum f = \sum_k f(2k) + \sum_k f(2k+1). The constant family f≡1f \equiv 1 is its own even part and its own odd part, so its sum SS satisfies S=S+SS = S + S, hence S=0S = 0. But any sum worth the name lies above the finite sections of a nonnegative family, and the first section here is 11, so 1≤01 \leq 0. The prototype file SeriesLab.v at the repository root proves exactly this contradiction (unconditional_parity_is_false), which is why the class reads:

  Class Series (A : Type) `{Ring A}
      {Ss : HasSsum A} {Sm : HasSummable A}
      (bigop_list : forall J : Type, (J -> A) -> list J -> A)
      {BL : BigOp A bigop_list} := {
    ssum_proper     :: Proper (equ ==> equ) Ss;
    summable_proper :: Proper (equ ==> iff) Sm;

    summable_add    : forall f g : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable g -> summable (fun n => f n + g n);
    summable_neg    : forall f : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable (fun n => - f n);
    summable_even   : forall f : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable (fun k => f (2 * k)%nat);
    summable_odd    : forall f : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable (fun k => f (S (2 * k)%nat));
    summable_cauchy : forall f g : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable g ->
      summable (fun n =>
        bigop_list _ (fun k => f k * g (n - k)%nat) (seq 0 (S n)));

    sum_supported : forall (f : nat -> A) (n : nat),
      (forall m : nat, (n <= m)%nat -> f m == 0) ->
      ssum f == bigop_list _ f (seq 0 n);
    sum_add       : forall f g : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable g ->
      ssum (fun n => f n + g n) == ssum f + ssum g;
    sum_neg       : forall f : nat -> A,
      summable f -> ssum (fun n => - f n) == - ssum f;
    sum_parity    : forall f : nat -> A,
      summable f ->
      ssum f == ssum (fun k => f (2 * k)%nat) + ssum (fun k => f (S (2 * k)%nat));
    sum_cauchy    : forall f g : nat -> A,
      summable f -> summable g ->
      ssum f * ssum g ==
        ssum (fun n =>
          bigop_list _ (fun k => f k * g (n - k)%nat) (seq 0 (S n)));
  }.

bigop_list f (seq 0 n) is the finite section f(0)+⋯+f(n−1)f(0) + \cdots + f(n-1), and sum_supported is what ties ssum to it: a family that vanishes from nn on has its nn-th section as its sum. The finite sum enters as a class parameter carrying its two equations rather than as a fixed function, so that these laws are about a summation and not about one particular way of computing one. The closure fields (summable_add and its four siblings) are there because a proof that rearranges a sum must know the rearranged family is still in the domain. A second class, ScaleSeries, relates summation on an algebra to summation on its scalars: sum_scale pulls a scalar out of a sum of vectors, and sum_scale_r turns a family of multiples of one vector into a scalar sum times that vector.

The trig instance, which never unfolds the sum

Impl/TrigSeries.v defines the three operations by the usual series and derives all six laws from the class above. Nothing in it mentions an epsilon, a partial sum or a limit. The terms are written as the recursions that define them — xn/n!x^{n}/n! one step at a time, dividing by the image of nn under the unique ring map ℕ→F\mathbb{N} \to F:

  Fixpoint exp_term (x : A) (n : nat) : A :=
    match n with
    | O => 1
    | S m => inv (from_nat (S m)) *: (x * exp_term x m)
    end.

with cos_term t k the two-step recursion for (−1)kt2k/(2k)!(-1)^k t^{2k}/(2k)! and sin_term t k the one for (−1)kt2k+1/(2k+1)!(-1)^k t^{2k+1}/(2k+1)!, and

  Definition cexp_sum (x : A) : A := ssum (exp_term x).
  Definition cos_sum (t : F) : F := ssum (cos_term t).
  Definition sin_sum (t : F) : F := ssum (sin_term t).

The analytic input is three hypotheses — that the three families are summable — plus one about the scalars: from_nat_neq_0, characteristic zero, without which the divided powers do not exist.

The exponential law is the Cauchy product read backwards. The coefficient identity is the binomial theorem, proved by induction on nn as dexp_binomial:

∑k=0nxkk!yn−k(n−k)!=(x+y)nn!(xy=yx),\sum_{k=0}^{n} \frac{x^k}{k!}\,\frac{y^{n-k}}{(n-k)!} = \frac{(x+y)^n}{n!} \qquad (xy = yx),

and with it the law is two steps:

  Theorem series_cexp_add : forall x y : A, x * y == y * x ->
    cexp_sum (x + y) == cexp_sum x * cexp_sum y.
  Proof.
    intros x y Hxy.
    calc (ssum (exp_term (x + y)))
      == (ssum (fun n => bigop (S n)
                  (fun k => exp_term x k * exp_term y (n - k)%nat)))
           { (ssum_proper _ _
                (fun n => symmetry (dexp_binomial x y Hxy n))) }
      == (ssum (exp_term x) * ssum (exp_term y))
           { (sum_cauchy (exp_term x) (exp_term y)
                (exp_summable x) (exp_summable y)) }.
  Qed.

The first step rewrites under the sum — ssum_proper is the field saying that is allowed, and it needs the two families to agree termwise, which is dexp_binomial at each nn. The second is sum_cauchy read right to left, with the two summability hypotheses supplied. No property of ssum beyond those two is used.

Euler’s identity is the parity split. On the blackboard, at any ee with e2=−1e^2 = -1:

∑n(te)nn!=∑k(te)2k(2k)!+∑k(te)2k+1(2k+1)!{parity}\sum_{n} \frac{(t e)^n}{n!} = \sum_{k} \frac{(t e)^{2k}}{(2k)!} + \sum_{k} \frac{(t e)^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!} \quad \{\text{parity}\}

=∑k(−1)kt2k(2k)!⋅1+∑k(−1)kt2k+1(2k+1)!⋅e{e2=−1 termwise; scalars out}= \sum_{k} \frac{(-1)^k t^{2k}}{(2k)!}\cdot 1 + \sum_{k} \frac{(-1)^k t^{2k+1}}{(2k+1)!}\cdot e \quad \{e^2 = -1 \text{ termwise; scalars out}\}

and in Coq the same five steps:

  Theorem series_euler : forall (t : F) (e : A), e * e == - (1) ->
    cexp_sum (t *: e) == cos_sum t *: (1 : A) + sin_sum t *: e.
  Proof.
    intros t e He.
    calc (ssum (exp_term (t *: e)))
      == (ssum (fun k => exp_term (t *: e) (2 * k)%nat)
            + ssum (fun k => exp_term (t *: e) (S (2 * k)%nat)))
           { (sum_parity (exp_term (t *: e)) (exp_summable (t *: e))) }
      == (ssum (fun k => cos_term t k *: (1 : A))
            + ssum (fun k => exp_term (t *: e) (S (2 * k)%nat)))
           { (ssum_proper _ _ (euler_even t e He)) }
      == (ssum (fun k => cos_term t k *: (1 : A))
            + ssum (fun k => sin_term t k *: e))
           { (ssum_proper _ _ (euler_odd t e He)) }
      == (ssum (cos_term t) *: (1 : A) + ssum (fun k => sin_term t k *: e))
           { (sum_scale_r (cos_term t) (1 : A) (cos_summable t)) }
      == (ssum (cos_term t) *: (1 : A) + ssum (sin_term t) *: e)
           { (sum_scale_r (sin_term t) e (sin_summable t)) }.
  Qed.

Step one is sum_parity with its guard. Steps two and three are the termwise identities euler_even and euler_odd, each an induction whose step is one turn of the Euler line: two steps of the exponential series multiply the coefficient by −t2-t^2 over the two factors the index passes, and leave the direction — 11 or ee — where it was (exp_term_line_step). Steps four and five pull the scalar sums out by sum_scale_r.

Pythagoras is then a consequence rather than a third series argument: multiply cexp(t⋅e)\mathrm{cexp}(t\cdot e) by cexp((−t)⋅e)\mathrm{cexp}((-t)\cdot e), which is cexp(0)=1\mathrm{cexp}(0) = 1 by the addition law, and expand both factors by Euler and the plane’s multiplication table. Pythagoras is a statement about the scalars, so two more hypotheses are needed to reach them, and they are stated where they are used: the algebra has a square root of −1-1, and it embeds the scalars faithfully. Both hold in every rotation algebra of the previous sections.

  Context (imaginary : A)
          (imaginary_square : imaginary * imaginary == - (1))
          (scale_one_injective :
             forall a b : F, a *: (1 : A) == b *: (1 : A) -> a == b).
  Theorem series_pythagoras : forall t : F,
    cos_sum t * cos_sum t + sin_sum t * sin_sum t == 1.

The six fields then assemble into TrigSeries_Circular, an instance over any algebra meeting the hypotheses — and everything in the previous section applies to it unchanged.

What is still owed

One field of the class is not reachable from any equation: sin_nontrivial. Over an ordered field it needs an estimate — 0<sin⁡10 < \sin 1, say — so Impl/TrigSeries.v takes it as a hypothesis beside the summability ones and says so:

  Context (sine_nontrivial : exists t : F, sin_sum t =/= 0).

Discharging that hypothesis and the three summability ones means building a Series instance over a complete ordered field. Impl/SeriesReals.v does that. It starts from the sum relation — SumTo f L reads “the finite sections of f converge to L”:

  Definition SumTo (f : nat -> R) (L : R) : Prop :=
    seq_lim (fun n => bigop n f) L.

Uniqueness of the limit, linearity, and the sum of a finitely supported family are proved for an arbitrary ordered field — completeness is never used, and Coq’s generalization records it. Past that line the file crosses two thresholds, both named in its header. sections_lub applies completeness to the set of finite sections; lub_section_index turns “s−εs - \varepsilon is not an upper bound of them” into the section that exceeds it, which is Classical_Prop.classic — an ordered field decides each single comparison by le_total, but nothing in it turns a negated universal over the indices into a witness. Monotone convergence follows, and with it sum_exists_abs_summable, since an absolutely summable family is the difference of two increasing ones, term by term:

    apply (sum_to_ext (fun i => (abs (f i) + f i) + - abs (f i)) f).

sum_to_parity is then a reindexing — a section of even length is the pair of half-length sections, and those are a subsequence of all the sections — and sum_to_cauchy is Mertens’ theorem, whose section of the Cauchy product is the triangle of index pairs read by rows. The second threshold is the packaging: a total ssum : (nat -> R) -> R is data, so it is read off an existence proof by IndefiniteDescription.constructive_indefinite_description, against a specification with two clauses — the sum where there is one, and 0 where there is none, which is what makes it a function of the family and not of a proof. reals_series is the instance, and Print Assumptions in the file checks that those two axioms are all it uses.

sin_nontrivial is discharged in Impl/TrigReals.v by one estimate: the terms of sin_term 1 shrink by at most a quarter at each step, because the two-step recursion divides by two consecutive naturals from 2 on. That is summability by the ratio test, and it also makes the tail from index one on smaller than the first term, which is 1:

  Theorem sin_one_positive : 0 < sin_sum (1 : R).

What is left before Circular holds over the reals outright is the same estimate at an arbitrary argument, and an algebra to hold the exponential. The ratio for exp_term x falls below one only past an index that depends on x, so the ratio test has to start there, and finding the index is the Archimedean property. And euler and pythagoras speak about an algebra with a square root of −1-1, which over the reals means the complex numbers carrying a Series instance of their own — summability component by component, and a Cauchy product that mixes the components.
