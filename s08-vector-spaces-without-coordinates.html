Vector spaces without coordinates

A field is a set of scalars you can add, multiply, and — except for zero — divide. The rational numbers ℚ\mathbb{Q} and the real numbers ℝ\mathbb{R} are the standard examples: addition and multiplication each have an identity (00 and 11), negation undoes addition everywhere, reciprocals undo multiplication everywhere except at 00, and multiplication distributes over addition. Everything below sits on top of one fixed field FF, whose elements we will call scalars.

One notational point first. Throughout, == is not Coq’s built-in equality of terms but a setoid equality: a chosen equivalence relation that every operation is required to respect. For ℚ\mathbb{Q} that relation makes 1/2 and 2/4 the same element; for the abstract structures here it is whatever equality each carrier comes with.

A vector space is an abelian group acted on by a field

The vectors themselves form an abelian group: they add, addition is associative and commutative, there is a zero vector, and every vector has a negative. On top of that group, the field acts by scaling — a scalar aa and a vector uu produce a vector a*:ua *: u — subject to four laws: scaling distributes over vector sums and over scalar sums, two scalings compose by multiplying the scalars, and the scalar 11 scales trivially.

In the code those four laws are a small mixin, ScaleLaws, kept separate from the group so that other structures can reuse it over a group they already own:

Module ScaleLaws.
  Class ScaleLaws (F V : Type) `{Field F}
      {Ve : EqRel V} {Va : HasAdd V} {Vs : HasScale F V} := {
    scale_proper          :: Proper (equ ==> equ ==> equ) Vs;
    is_left_distributive  :: LeftDistributive F V V Vs;
    is_right_distributive :: RightDistributive F V V Vs;
    is_associative        :: Associative F V Vs mult;
    is_left_identity      :: LeftIdentity F V Vs 1;
  }.
  Hint Mode ScaleLaws - ! - - - - - - - - - - - : typeclass_instances.
End ScaleLaws.
Export ScaleLaws.

The four laws are the four promised above, and not one of them is written out as an equation. Each is a class from Props.v — the same classes the additive structures are built from — applied to a scalar acting on a vector:

  • LeftDistributive F V V Vs is a *: (u + v) == a *: u + a *: v: an operation taking an F and a V to a V, distributing over a sum on its right.
  • RightDistributive F V V Vs is (a + b) *: u == a *: u + b *: u, where the sum being split is a sum of scalars.
  • Associative F V Vs mult is (a * b) *: u == a *: (b *: u). The associativity class takes an action and an operation separately; a monoid uses it with both slots filled by the same +, and here the action is scaling and the operation is the field’s *.
  • LeftIdentity F V Vs 1 is 1 *: u == u, the identity law at that same action, with the field’s 1 as the unit.

The remaining field, ScaleLaws.scale_proper, is the “every operation is required to respect ==” clause: scaling an equal vector by an equal scalar gives an equal result. All five are declared with :: rather than :, so each is a typeclass instance as soon as a ScaleLaws is in scope, and a proof cites the law’s own method — associative, left_identity — rather than the field.

A vector space is then those laws together with an abelian group of vectors. Two fields, nothing else:

Module VectorSpace.
  Class VectorSpace (F V : Type) `{Field F}
      {Ve : EqRel V} {Vz : Zero V} {Va : HasAdd V} {Vn : HasNeg V}
      {Vs : HasScale F V} := {
    vectors :: AbGroup V;
    scaling :: ScaleLaws F V;
  }.
  Hint Mode VectorSpace - ! - - - - - - - - - - - - - : typeclass_instances.
End VectorSpace.
Export VectorSpace.

No basis, no coordinates, no list of numbers — a vector space is those two halves over operations that arrive as class parameters (Ve through Vs), and nothing more. Everything provable about vector spaces in general is provable from exactly this. Because both fields are declared with ::, the halves come apart again by projection whenever a proof wants one: a theorem about abelian groups applies to the vectors with nothing said, and a theorem about scaling applies without the group being mentioned. There is no separate instance recording that a vector space is an abelian group, and no assembly function for building one out of its parts — the parts are the fields, so a caller writes the record with its two halves in it.

Scaling commutes: scale_swap

Here is a small consequence of the laws, proved for every vector space at once. Scaling by aa and then by bb gives the same vector as scaling by bb and then by aa:

a*:(b*:u)=(ab)*:u{scalings fuse}=(ba)*:u{scalars commute}=b*:(a*:u){scalings split}a \mathbin{*:} (b \mathbin{*:} u) \;=\; (a b) \mathbin{*:} u \quad\{\text{scalings fuse}\}\quad =\; (b a) \mathbin{*:} u \quad\{\text{scalars commute}\}\quad =\; b \mathbin{*:} (a \mathbin{*:} u) \quad\{\text{scalings split}\}

The two outer equalities collapse and re-expand a nested scaling into a single scalar factor; the middle one is just the field’s commutativity of multiplication. In Coq:

Theorem scale_swap : forall (a b : F) (u : V),
  a *: (b *: u) == b *: (a *: u).
Proof.
  intros a b u.
  calc (a *: (b *: u))
    == ((a * b) *: u)  { (associative a b u) }
    == ((b * a) *: u)  { (commutative a b) }
    == (b *: (a *: u)) { (associative b a u) }.
Qed.

Step by step:

  • intros a b u fixes two scalars and a vector.
  • calc opens an equational chain. Each line == y { … } claims the current expression equals y, justified by the one fact named in the brace, and Coq checks that the chain as a whole proves the goal.
  • associative a b u is the scaling law (ab)*:u=a*:(b*:u)(a b) *: u = a *: (b *: u), used here right-to-left to fuse the nested scaling into the single factor aba b. The goal is what picks it: the term mentions *:, so the Associative instance found is the scaling one and not the field’s or the vectors’ own.
  • commutative a b is the field’s law ab=baa b = b a.
  • The same scaling law again, this time left-to-right, re-splits (ba)*:u(b a) *: u back into b*:(a*:u)b *: (a *: u).

The one fact doing the real work is commutative: scalars commute because the field’s multiplication does. Hold on to that — a page from now we will meet a multiplication that does not commute, and the contrast is the whole point.

Reuse: scaling preserves the zero vector

Not every proof needs its own induction. Fix a scalar aa and look at the map u↦a*:uu \mapsto a *: u. The left-distributive law says this map sends a sum to the sum of the images — that is exactly what it means to be a homomorphism of the additive group. And a general fact about groups, proved once for every map that preserves +, is that such a map must send 00 to 00; the lemma is called hom_zero. So scaling a zero vector gives zero, with no new work:

Theorem scale_zero : forall a : F, a *: (0 : V) == 0.
Proof.
  intros a.
  exact (hom_zero (scale a) (left_distributive a)).
Qed.

The proof is a single exact: hand hom_zero the map (scale a) and its additivity law — which is left_distributive at this scalar, the class method resolving to the ScaleLaws instance because the term is built from *: — and the group-level result applies verbatim. The fact that scale a respects == isn’t passed by hand at all, it’s found automatically (scale_l_proper, registered as an instance right above this theorem in the source, is exactly that fact). This is the payoff of proving things about structures rather than about particular types: a lemma about groups is already a lemma about the additive part of every vector space.

A space of transformations, one level up

Now the twist that gives this post its title. Among all functions between two vector spaces UU and VV, the interesting ones are the linear maps: those that respect ==, send sums to sums, and commute with scaling. In the code a linear map is a record carrying its function together with those three guarantees:

Record LinearMap (F U V : Type)
    {eU : EqRel U} {aU : HasAdd U} {sU : HasScale F U}
    {eV : EqRel V} {aV : HasAdd V} {sV : HasScale F V} := {
  lm_fun :> U -> V;
  lm_linear :: Linear F U V lm_fun;
}.

The record asks for surprisingly little: each carrier needs an equality, an addition and a scaling, and nothing in the record’s type requires them to be vector spaces — that hypothesis appears only in the theorems that actually use it. lm_fun is declared with :>, which makes it a coercion, so a LinearMap can be applied to a vector directly and f u means lm_fun f u.

The other field says the function is linear; linearity itself is a separate class, stated about a plain function rather than about a bundle:

Module Linear.
  Class Linear (F U V : Type)
      {eU : EqRel U} {aU : HasAdd U} {sU : HasScale F U}
      {eV : EqRel V} {aV : HasAdd V} {sV : HasScale F V}
      (f : U -> V) := {
    proper         :: Proper (equ ==> equ) f;
    is_additive    :: Additive U V add add f;
    is_homogeneous :: Homogeneous F U V scale (fun a => a) f;
  }.
  Hint Mode Linear - - - - - - - - - ! : typeclass_instances.
End Linear.
Export Linear.

Additive and Homogeneous are the two halves, each a single law about a map, and both live in Props.v beside Associative and Injective. Additive U V add add f is f (x + y) == f x + f y, and it takes two operations because the sum on the left lives in U and the sum on the right lives in V. Homogeneous F U V scale (fun a => a) f is f (a *: x) == weight a *: f x with the weight taken to be the identity function: a linear map scales by exactly the scalar it was handed. The weight is a parameter because not every map does — a norm satisfies the same law with weight abs, a quadratic form with weight fun a => a * a — and stating it once with a weight is what lets those share the class.

Splitting them out means a lambda written anywhere can be shown additive without first being packaged into a LinearMap — and a LinearMap’s three laws are still reachable under the names linear_map_proper, linear_map_additive and linear_map_scale.

Write Hom U V for the collection of these maps. Here is the fact: the linear maps between UU and VV are themselves a vector space. You can add two maps (u↦fu+guu \mapsto f\,u + g\,u), negate a map, and scale a map by a scalar (u↦a*:fuu \mapsto a *: f\,u) — all pointwise — and each result is again linear, and the vector-space laws hold at every point because they hold in VV.

Take the scaling of a map. To register it as a linear map we owe a proof that a*:fa *: f commutes with scaling — and that proof is scale_swap, the theorem above, applied at each point:

Lemma hom_smul_scale : forall (b : F) (u : U),
  a *: f (b *: u) == b *: (a *: f u).
Proof.
  intros b u.
  rewrite (linear_map_scale f b u).
  apply scale_swap.
Qed.

rewrite (linear_map_scale f b u) pushes the inner scaling through ff; what remains, a*:(b*:fu)=b*:(a*:fu)a *: (b *: f\,u) = b *: (a *: f\,u), is scale_swap. Four laws like it make up the scaling half of the structure:

  #[global] Instance Hom_ScaleLaws : ScaleLaws F Hom :=
    {| ScaleLaws.scale_proper := hom_scale_proper;
       ScaleLaws.is_left_distributive  := hom_scale_add;
       ScaleLaws.is_right_distributive := hom_add_scale;
       ScaleLaws.is_associative        := hom_scale_scale;
       ScaleLaws.is_left_identity      := hom_scale_1 |}.

and the vector space of maps is that half beside the pointwise abelian group, in a record with two fields:

  #[global] Instance Hom_VectorSpace : VectorSpace F Hom :=
    {| VectorSpace.vectors := Hom_AbGroup; VectorSpace.scaling := Hom_ScaleLaws |}.

Both records name every field explicitly instead of leaving {} for instance search to fill, and the file says why: searching for something like Monoid ?T with the carrier still unknown sends Coq hunting through every parametric instance in scope, including the pointwise ones on function types, and it does not come back. Naming the field gives search a carrier to work from.

A space of transformations has its own vector-space structure, one level up — built out of the space it maps into, by lifting each law through “evaluate at a point.”

The endomorphism ring: composition does not commute

Specialize to the maps from a space to itself, EndV=HomVV\mathrm{End}\,V = \mathrm{Hom}\,V\,V. These have everything above — they add and scale as a vector space — but they carry one operation more: you can compose two of them. Composition takes the role of multiplication, with the identity map as the unit 11:

  #[global] Instance End_One : One EndV := linear_map_id.

  #[global] Instance End_Mult : HasMult EndV :=
    fun f g : EndV => linear_map_comp f g.

linear_map_comp g f is the map applying g after f, so f * g means “f after g”, and the unit is the identity map.

With addition from the vector-space structure and composition as the product, EndV\mathrm{End}\,V satisfies the ring laws — composition is associative, the identity map is a two-sided unit, and composition distributes over addition on both sides:

f∘(g+h)=f∘g+f∘h,(f+g)∘h=f∘h+g∘hf\circ(g+h) = f\circ g + f\circ h, \qquad (f+g)\circ h = f\circ h + g\circ h

Those two are worth looking at, because they are not symmetric:

  Lemma end_distl : forall f g h : EndV, f * (g + h) == f * g + f * h.
  Proof.
    intros f g h u.
    apply (linear_map_additive f).
  Qed.

  Lemma end_distr : forall f g h : EndV, (f + g) * h == f * h + g * h.
  Proof.
    intros f g h u.
    reflexivity.
  Qed.

Two maps are equal when they agree at every point, so both proofs open with intros f g h u, naming a point u at which to compare. On the left the two sides are f (g u + h u) and f (g u) + f (h u), which differ by whether f is applied before or after the sum — so the law is f’s own additivity, and linear_map_additive f closes it. On the right both sides read f (h u) + g (h u) already, and reflexivity is the whole proof: distributing on that side is bookkeeping about which map is applied first, not a fact about linearity.

The ring instance itself, End_Ring, is one nested record literal: the abelian group is the pointwise one proved for Hom, the multiplicative monoid is composition with linear_map_id as its unit, and the last two fields are the pair of lemmas above. That the assembly really type-checks as a ring is recorded in the file as a one-line check, whose proof is an underscore — “find it”:

Example end_ring_check (F V : Type) `{FF : Field F}
  {eV : EqRel V} {zV : Zero V} {aV : HasAdd V} {nV : HasNeg V} {sV : HasScale F V} {VV : VectorSpace F V} :
  Ring (LinearMap F V V) := _.

This ring is noncommutative, and the reason is the one noted earlier. Scalar multiplication commuted because the field’s multiplication did — scale_swap had commutative at its centre. Composition has no such law: f*gf * g means “ff after gg”, and f(gu)f(g\,u) is not in general g(fu)g(f\,u). Nothing is broken; the axioms a ring demands (associativity, units, distributivity) never included commutativity of the product, precisely so that structures like this one qualify.

And because EndV\mathrm{End}\,V is registered as a ring, every theorem proved once for all rings applies to it with no further work. Here is one, quoted from the file:

Example end_mul_0_l (F V : Type) `{FF : Field F} `{VV : VectorSpace F V} :
  forall f : LinearMap F V V, 0 * f == 0.
Proof.
  intros f.
  exact (mul_0_l f).
Qed.

mul_0_l — 0⋅f=00 \cdot f = 0, zero times anything is zero — is a generic ring lemma. For EndV\mathrm{End}\,V it says the zero map composed after any ff is again the zero map, and its proof here is one exact: the general theorem, instantiated. Prove the ring laws once, inherit the ring theory everywhere.
