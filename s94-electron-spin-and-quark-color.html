Electron spin’s double cover, and quark color’s two quantum numbers

Standard quantum mechanics introduces an electron’s spin as a bare postulate. A textbook states that the electron carries an intrinsic angular momentum of one-half a fundamental unit, calls this “spin one-half,” and moves on to using the fact — the number 1/21/2 is simply handed down, not derived from anything smaller. One of its stranger consequences is presented the same way: a spin-1/2 particle’s quantum state has to be rotated a full 720°720°, not 360°360°, before it returns to where it started, and a student is given this rule with no visual or algebraic reason for why 720720 rather than 360360. The equations that govern spin — the Pauli equation, and the Schrödinger equation behind it — contain the imaginary unit ii, and the usual justification for its presence is that the equations do not work without it, not any account of what a complex number is doing inside a description of a spinning particle. The Pauli matrices, the three 2×22\times2 tables that encode spin’s algebra, are typically memorized as a set of anticommutation relations rather than derived from a smaller set of assumptions. None of this is a strawman: it is how the material is actually taught, a working rule with its geometric reason stripped out.

A separate question concerns quarks. A quark carries a property called color charge, with three possible values, and gluons — the particles that carry the strong force between quarks — are tied to that same threefold structure. Where does the number three come from, and is there any geometric reason behind it? Is there a reason a quark needs a color label at all, separate from spin, and does the group that organizes color charge, written SU(3)SU(3), have to have the particular structure it has?

Both questions have partial answers once vectors are allowed to multiply.

Multiplying vectors, and a real square root of −1

A geometric algebra multiplies vectors, distributing over addition but not assumed to commute, subject to one governing law: a vector’s square is a plain scalar, its own squared length, vv=‖v‖2v\,v = \lVert v\rVert^2. Splitting the product of two different vectors into its symmetric and antisymmetric halves recovers the ordinary inner product in the symmetric half, and produces a new kind of object, a bivector, in the antisymmetric half whenever the two vectors are independent.

Three-dimensional space generates the algebra Cl(3,0)\mathrm{Cl}(3,0): three vectors e1,e2,e3e_1, e_2, e_3, each squaring to 11 and pairwise anticommuting (eiej=−ejeie_ie_j = -e_je_i for i≠ji\ne j), together with everything their products generate. That closes off at eight dimensions: the scalar 11; the three vectors e1,e2,e3e_1,e_2,e_3; the three bivectors e12,e13,e23e_{12},e_{13},e_{23} (products of two distinct generators); and one further object, the trivector e123=e1e2e3e_{123}=e_1e_2e_3. The even part — everything built from an even number of vector factors, spanned by {1,e12,e13,e23}\{1,e_{12},e_{13},e_{23}\} — is closed under multiplication on its own: multiplying two even elements always produces another even element.

The governing law itself is a proved theorem, not an axiom of one hand-built multiplication table: the implementation (Impl/Clifford.v) constructs the Clifford algebra of any number of generators from a signature listing each generator’s square, and proves once, for every dimension and every signature, that an embedded vector squares to its own quadratic form on the unit (gv_square). At three generators of square 11 — the signature sig3 — that instance reads:

  Example cl3_contraction : forall v : Fin.t 3 -> F,
    cl_mul sig3 (ga_vec v) (ga_vec v) == qform sig3 v *: cl_one.
  Proof. exact (gv_square 3 sig3). Qed.

ga_vec v embeds the coordinate triple vv as a grade-1 element, cl_mul sig3 is the geometric product, and qform sig3 v is v12+v22+v32v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2 — the vector’s squared length, sitting as a plain number on the unit cl_one.

Where, in all this real geometry, is a square root of −1-1? On the bivectors. Take two orthonormal generators e1,e2e_1, e_2 and square their bivector:

(e1e2)(e1e2)=e1(e2e1)e2=−e1(e1e2)e2=−(e1e1)(e2e2)=−1,(e_1e_2)(e_1e_2) \;=\; e_1(e_2e_1)e_2 \;=\; -\,e_1(e_1e_2)e_2 \;=\; -\,(e_1e_1)(e_2e_2) \;=\; -1,

using only associativity, the anticommutation of two orthogonal vectors, and each vector’s own square. Every step of that chain is mechanized in the two-generator instance of the same construction, where e1 and e2 abbreviate the two embedded basis vectors. The ingredients first — each derived from the contraction law, not read off a table: e1 * e1 == 1 comes from the contraction at the first basis vector, and anticommutation comes from polarization, the symmetric half of the product of two vectors, which vanishes here because the two directions are orthogonal:

  Example e1_squared : e1 * e1 == 1.
  Example e1_e2_anticommute : e1 * e2 == - (e2 * e1).

Then the square itself, the blackboard chain above verbatim:

  Example e12_squared : (e1 * e2) * (e1 * e2) == - (1).
  Proof.
    transitivity ((e1 * (- e1)) : Cl F 2).
    - calc ((e1 * e2) * (e1 * e2))
        == ((e1 * (e2 * (e1 * e2))) : Cl F 2)
           { associative }
        == ((e1 * ((e2 * e1) * e2)) : Cl F 2)
           { associative }
        == ((e1 * ((- (e1 * e2)) * e2)) : Cl F 2)
           { e2_e1_anticommute }
        == ((e1 * (- ((e1 * e2) * e2))) : Cl F 2)
           { mul_neg_l }
        == ((e1 * (- (e1 * (e2 * e2)))) : Cl F 2)
           { associative }
        == ((e1 * (- (e1 * (1 : Cl F 2)))) : Cl F 2)
           { e2_squared }
        == ((e1 * (- e1)) : Cl F 2)
           { right_identity }.
    - calc ((e1 * (- e1)) : Cl F 2)
        == ((- (e1 * e1)) : Cl F 2)
           { mul_neg_r }
        == ((- (1)) : Cl F 2)
           { e1_squared }.
  Qed.

The calc chain is the blackboard proof step for step, each equation justified in braces: two regroupings by associative bring e2e_2 and e1e_1 together; e2_e1_anticommute swaps them at the cost of one minus sign; the sign is pulled out (mul_neg_l), one more regrouping exposes e2e2e_2e_2, which e2_squared collapses to 11; what remains is e1(−e1)e_1(-e_1), and the second chain pulls the sign out (mul_neg_r) and closes with e1_squared. A square root of −1-1, built out of two real, perpendicular unit directions and nothing else.

With a real, geometric square root of −1-1 available, a spin state can be represented without complex numbers at all. Following Hestenes (1967; Am. J. Phys., 1984) — a genuinely published account, textbook-adjacent even though most physicists still learn the complex-matrix version — the nonrelativistic electron’s spin state is a real even multivector, ψ=ρR\psi=\sqrt\rho\,R, where ρ\rho is a nonnegative scalar (a probability density) and RR is a unit even element, with multiplication by a fixed bivector standing wherever the standard Pauli equation multiplies by ii. (Hestenes’s own bookkeeping routes this through the trivector e123e_{123}, which also squares to −1-1 and commutes with everything — a classical fact about odd dimension, not re-proved here.) Reproducing the Pauli equation’s actual dynamics from this substitution is Hestenes’s result, not something derived from scratch below; what the algebra above supplies is the fact that makes the substitution legitimate in the first place — a real object that squares to −1-1, built from perpendicular directions rather than postulated.

Color charge as an abstract Lie algebra, and why rank two

A Lie algebra is a vector space with an extra operation, a bracket [x,y][x,y], that is bilinear in each argument, antisymmetric ([x,y]=−[y,x][x,y]=-[y,x]), and satisfies the Jacobi identity, [x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+[z,[x,y]]=0[x,[y,z]]+[y,[z,x]]+[z,[x,y]]=0 for every x,y,zx,y,z. Quark color charge is organized by one particular 8-dimensional Lie algebra, 𝔰𝔲(3)\mathfrak{su}(3): eight generators T1,…,T8T_1,\dots,T_8, and a bracket fixed by nine numbers, the structure constants:

The table is written out as a match on the three indices, one line per ordered triple, with everything not listed sent to zero. Here are the nine increasing-index representatives, one per unordered triple that carries a nonzero value:

  Definition sc (a b c : nat) : F :=
    match a, b, c with
  | 1, 2, 3 => 1
  …
  | 1, 4, 7 => half 1
  | 1, 5, 6 => - (half 1)
  | 2, 4, 6 => half 1
  | 2, 5, 7 => half 1
  | 3, 4, 5 => half 1
  | 3, 6, 7 => - (half 1)
  | 4, 5, 8 => half sqrt3
  | 6, 7, 8 => half sqrt3
  …
    | _, _, _ => 0
    end.

This is the standard table (Georgi, Lie Algebras in Particle Physics): f123=1f_{123}=1, four entries equal to 1/21/2, two equal to −1/2-1/2, and two equal to 3/2\sqrt3/2. Every other nonzero value of ff is one of these nine with its sign changed by however many swaps it takes to sort its three indices into increasing order, and each of those permuted entries is spelled out as its own match line rather than derived, so that the table computes. half 1 is the field’s 1/21/2; sqrt3 is not a real number here but a section hypothesis, an element of the field satisfying sqrt3 * sqrt3 == 1 + 1 + 1 — any ordered field admitting such an element instantiates everything below. The bracket itself is built directly from the table, as a double sum over the eight generators:

  Definition bracket (x y : Fin.t 8 -> F) : Fin.t 8 -> F :=
    fun c => bigopF (fun a => bigopF (fun b => x a * y b * f a b c)).

Where does the number three, and SU(3)SU(3)’s particular shape, actually enter? Not by finding a literal “3” anywhere in the table — a table like this simply is what 𝔰𝔲(3)\mathfrak{su}(3) means — but through a subtler fact: which pairs of generators commute with each other. T3T_3 and T8T_8 do:

f38c=0 for every output c, since no line of the table above pairs 3 with 8.f_{38c}=0 \text{ for every output } c, \text{ since no line of the table above pairs } 3 \text{ with } 8.

  Lemma su3_cartan_38 : bracket (e g3) (e g8) == 0.
  Proof.
    intros d.
    rewrite (bracket_e_e g3 g8 d).
    unfold f, gnum, fin_to_nat, sc; cbn.
    reflexivity.
  Qed.

(e i is the standard basis vector, 11 at coordinate ii and 00 elsewhere; g3, g8 name the third and eighth of the eight basis directions.) bracket_e_e (bracket (e i) (e j) d == f i j d) turns the bracket of two basis generators directly into a single structure-constant lookup; unfolding the table at 33 and 88 then finds nothing but zeros, for every output coordinate, closed by reflexivity.

A maximal set of mutually commuting generators is called a Cartan subalgebra, and its size is the algebra’s rank. T3T_3 and T8T_8 commuting is the algebraic fact behind SU(3)SU(3)’s rank being two: two, not one, color-type quantities can be given a definite value on the same state simultaneously. That is the actual content behind quarks needing three colors rather than one: a single label only distinguishes states along one measurable axis, while two independent, simultaneously measurable labels are what it takes to tell red, green, and blue apart as three distinct combinations of two numbers, rather than three points on one line.

No single Clifford algebra is currently known to contain the full Standard Model group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1)SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1) the way Cl(2,0)\mathrm{Cl}(2,0) contains U(1)U(1) and Cl(3,0)\mathrm{Cl}(3,0) contains SU(2)SU(2); 𝔰𝔲(3)\mathfrak{su}(3) here is built and checked on its own terms, as an abstract Lie algebra, with no embedding into any geometric algebra claimed.

The double cover: a spinor and its negation act identically

Producing a rotation from Cl(3,0)\mathrm{Cl}(3,0) needs one more operation: reversal, which reverses the order of the vector factors inside every blade. Reversing a product of kk vectors costs k(k−1)/2k(k-1)/2 transpositions of adjacent anticommuting factors, and that count is odd exactly when k∈{2,3}k\in\{2,3\} — so reversal negates exactly the bivector and trivector coefficients, and leaves the scalar and vector parts alone. The general definition applies that sign blade by blade, and at three generators it does exactly what the transposition count says, writing mk3 for the element with the eight listed coefficients:

  Lemma reverse_mk3 : forall c a1 a2 a3 p12 p13 p23 t : F,
    cl_reverse (mk3 c a1 a2 a3 p12 p13 p23 t)
    == mk3 c a1 a2 a3 (- p12) (- p13) (- p23) (- t).

Reversal turns a product around, xỹ=ỹx̃\widetilde{xy}=\tilde y\,\tilde x (reverse_anti3) — the one property that makes the next definition behave like a conjugation rather than an arbitrary sandwich. An element of the even subalgebra — scalar plus the three bivectors — is called a spinor:

  Definition spinor (r b12 b13 b23 : F) : Cl F 3 :=
    mk3 r 0 0 0 b12 b13 b23 0.

and the sandwich is:

  Definition rotate (R x : Cl F 3) : Cl F 3 := R * x * cl_reverse R.

Sandwiching a vector by an even element produces another vector — proved for every even RR, with no unit hypothesis RR̃=1R\,\tilde R=1 used anywhere in it:

  Lemma rotate_coordinates_correct : forall (R : Cl F 3) (v : Fin.t 3 -> F),
    cl_even R -> rotate R (ga_vec v) == ga_vec (rotate_coordinates R v).

Read as an action on vectors, that sandwich is a rotation; what follows is what it does when RR itself is negated.

Theorem. A spinor RR and its negation −R-R sandwich every vector to the identical result.

Reversal only ever rescales coefficients — it is linear — so reversing −R-R negates R̃\tilde R a second time on top of the first: −R̃=−R̃\widetilde{-R}=-\tilde R. Substituting that into the sandwich,

(−R)v(−R)̃=(−R)v(−R̃)=(−1)(−1)⋅RvR̃=RvR̃,(-R)\,v\,\widetilde{(-R)} \;=\; (-R)\,v\,(-\tilde R) \;=\; (-1)(-1)\cdot R\,v\,\tilde R \;=\; R\,v\,\tilde R,

the two minus signs produced by negating RR meet and cancel outright. The mechanized form of this fact is the sandwich’s coordinate formula, computed out in full for an arbitrary spinor and an arbitrary vector:

  Lemma rotate_spinor_vec : forall r b12 b13 b23 v1 v2 v3 : F,
    rotate (spinor r b12 b13 b23) (ga_vec (space_vector v1 v2 v3))
    == ga_vec (space_vector
         ((r*r + - (b12*b12) + - (b13*b13) + b23*b23) * v1
          + (r*b12 + r*b12 + - (b13*b23 + b13*b23)) * v2
          + (r*b13 + r*b13 + b12*b23 + b12*b23) * v3)
         ((- (r*b12 + r*b12) + - (b13*b23 + b13*b23)) * v1
          + (r*r + - (b12*b12) + b13*b13 + - (b23*b23)) * v2
          + (r*b23 + r*b23 + - (b12*b13 + b12*b13)) * v3)
         ((b12*b23 + b12*b23 + - (r*b13 + r*b13)) * v1
          + (- (r*b23 + r*b23) + - (b12*b13 + b12*b13)) * v2
          + (r*r + b12*b12 + - (b13*b13) + - (b23*b23)) * v3)).

The right-hand side is a 3×33\times 3 rotation matrix written out entry by entry, and the double cover can be read directly off its shape: every summand in every entry is a product of exactly two of the spinor’s four coefficients — rrr\,r, rb12r\,b_{12}, b13b23b_{13}b_{23}, never a bare coefficient or a triple. Substituting (−r,−b12,−b13,−b23)(-r,-b_{12},-b_{13},-b_{23}) for (r,b12,b13,b23)(r,b_{12},b_{13},b_{23}) puts two minus signs into each summand, which cancel — every entry, and therefore the rotated vector, is unchanged. That is the (−1)(−1)=1(-1)(-1)=1 step of the blackboard argument, made visible in the theorem’s own statement.

Sandwiching by RR and by −R-R therefore act on every vector completely indistinguishably. Read as a statement about rotating three-dimensional vectors, this is the fact behind the rule stated without derivation in the opening section: the spinor generating a given spatial rotation is not unique, RR and −R-R both do it, so a continuous path of spinors from 11 back to a 360°360° rotation does not have to return to 11 — it can return to −1-1 instead, and only a second 360°360° turn, 720°720° total, is guaranteed to close the loop.

The Jacobi identity, checked one basis triple at a time

The bracket above is built directly from nine numbers, with no guarantee from that construction alone that it satisfies a Lie algebra’s defining laws. Bilinearity is a matter of distributing the double sum (bracket_add_l, bracket_add_r, bracket_scale_l, bracket_scale_r), and antisymmetry follows from the table’s own antisymmetry under swapping its first two indices (bracket_antisym, via f_antisym); both hold for arbitrary elements, not only basis vectors. The Jacobi identity is the one law that does not fall out so directly; whether these particular nine numbers genuinely combine into a consistent Lie algebra has to be checked. Define the Jacobiator of three elements as

  Definition jacobiator (x y z : Fin.t 8 -> F) : Fin.t 8 -> F :=
    bracket x (bracket y z) + bracket y (bracket z x) + bracket z (bracket x y).

and, for basis generators, jacobi_basis_expand rewrites each double bracket using the structure constants, turning the Jacobiator’s value at output coordinate dd into a sum of three single sums, one per cyclic pair.

Theorem (jacobi_123). The Jacobiator of T1,T2,T3T_1,T_2,T_3 vanishes at every output coordinate.

On the blackboard: the only nonzero value of ff with first two indices 2,32,3 is f231=1f_{231}=1, so the first of the three sums collapses to its lone term, at b=1b=1: f231f11d=f11df_{231}\,f_{11d}=f_{11d}. The same collapse happens to the other two sums, at b=2b=2 and b=3b=3 respectively, giving f22df_{22d} and f33df_{33d}. But every structure constant with a repeated index is zero — the table’s antisymmetry under swapping its first two arguments forces faad=−faadf_{aad}=-f_{aad}, hence faad=0f_{aad}=0 — so all three terms are individually zero, and the Jacobiator vanishes at T1,T2,T3T_1,T_2,T_3 not by a cancellation among several nonzero numbers but because each term is zero on its own. The Coq proof, verbatim:

  Lemma jacobi_123 : jacobiator (e g1) (e g2) (e g3) == 0.
  Proof. su3_jacobi g1 g2 g3. Qed.

su3_jacobi case-splits on the output coordinate dd (fin8_rect, an eight-way case analysis standing in for forall d : Fin.t 8), rewrites by jacobi_basis_expand to produce the three-term sum above, unfolds the table (sc) and the numbering (gnum), and simplifies (cbn) — turning the abstract sum into the concrete numbers the table actually stores. su3_step then clears the resulting zero terms with the ring facts mul_0_l/mul_0_r and closes each of the eight resulting goals, one per output coordinate, by reflexivity.

The same check is repeated, jacobi_124 through jacobi_678, for all (83)=56\binom{8}{3}=56 strictly increasing basis triples — every one closed, none Admitted. Fifty-two of them close on the table’s zeros and ordinary ring rearrangement, as jacobi_123 does. The remaining four are the triples drawn from T4T_4 through T7T_7, whose internal sums run through the two irrational entries f458=f678=3/2f_{458}=f_{678}=\sqrt3/2; they need one fact beyond the zeros, half_sqrt3_sq, that (3/2)2(\sqrt3/2)^2 is three copies of (1/2)2(1/2)^2, since 32=3\sqrt3^2=3.

What this does not explain

Geometric algebra does not explain the Pauli exclusion principle. The fact that two electrons cannot occupy the same quantum state — the antisymmetry of a fermion wavefunction under exchanging two particles — is a separate result, the spin-statistics theorem (Fierz 1939; Pauli, Phys. Rev. 58 (1940) 716), derived in relativistic quantum field theory from locality, Lorentz invariance, and a positive-definite energy spectrum. It has nothing to do with Cl(3,0)\mathrm{Cl}(3,0)’s spinor structure. What this post’s algebra explains is spin’s geometry: a bivector’s role as a real, geometric square root of −1-1, and the double cover, RR and −R-R producing the identical rotation. It does not explain, and does not attempt to explain, why electrons exclude one another.

Using the two algebras

Both constructions are ordinary algebra, computable directly. A spin state built as ρR\sqrt\rho\,R is arithmetic in the eight real coordinates of Cl(3,0)\mathrm{Cl}(3,0) — no complex matrices, and every operation above (rotate, cl_reverse, the double cover) computes the same way for any field, not only the reals. A color-charge computation is arithmetic in the eight real coordinates of 𝔰𝔲(3)\mathfrak{su}(3): given any two color states as vectors in Fin.t 8 -> F, bracket returns their commutator’s eight coordinates directly from the nine structure constants — a Lie bracket checked against its own defining laws, not assumed to satisfy them.
