Symmetry groups live inside geometric algebras

The problem: where does a rotation actually live?

Take a flat plane and turn it around a fixed point by some angle. This is a transformation: a rule sending every point of the plane to another point, leaving distances and the center point alone. Do two such turns one after another and the result is again a turn, by the sum of the two angles. Turning by zero does nothing, and turning by −θ-\theta after θ\theta undoes it. Closure under composition, an identity, and an inverse for every element are exactly the three laws that make “all the turns of a plane” a group — the same three laws that any collection of symmetries obeys, whether the symmetries are turns of a plane, turns of a cube, or rearrangements of a deck of cards.

But “a group” is only a list of laws the elements must obey. It says nothing about what the elements are, as things one could write down and compute with. The standard answer from a first linear-algebra course is: represent each turn by a matrix, and compose two turns by multiplying the matrices. That works, but the representation is borrowed rather than native — matrices were built for linear maps of every kind, and a 2×22\times 2 rotation matrix stores four numbers to record what is really one number, an angle.

Suppose instead that the plane’s own directed line segments — its vectors — could be multiplied, not just added and scaled, by a rule general enough to make sense in any number of dimensions. Would restricting that multiplication to the right subset of the results reproduce the turns of the plane exactly, with two turns composing by nothing more than ordinary multiplication of two such objects? And in three dimensions, where a turn needs an axis as well as an angle, does the same idea produce the turns of space, together with a fact that has no counterpart in the plane at all — that turning something by a full circle and not turning it at all are, at the level of the object doing the turning, two different operations? Both questions get exact, checked answers below.

Multiplying vectors: the geometric product, briefly

A vector space’s vectors add and scale; nothing so far lets two of them multiply. A geometric algebra adds exactly one such multiplication: the vectors are embedded into a ring — addition and multiplication, the latter not required to commute — subject to a single rule, that a vector multiplied by itself gives back its own squared length, evev=∥v∥2e_v e_v = \|v\|^2, sitting as a plain number on the ring’s unit. Splitting the product of two different vectors into its symmetric and antisymmetric halves recovers their ordinary inner product on the symmetric side, and on the antisymmetric side a new quantity built from nothing but the multiplication rule: an oriented plane element, a bivector, spanned by the two vectors. (Here == is the algebra’s own equality — a chosen equivalence relation every operation must respect — not Coq’s equality of raw terms.)

The smallest case with room for an actual rotation takes the vector space to be the ordinary plane, with two orthonormal vectors e1,e2e_1, e_2: e1e1=e2e2=1e_1e_1 = e_2e_2 = 1, and because the two are orthogonal, e1e2=−e2e1e_1e_2 = -e_2e_1. Multiplying pairs of these two vectors, and pairs of the results, closes up a four-dimensional ring — 1, e1e_1, e2e_2, and the bivector e12:=e1e2e_{12} := e_1e_2 — called Cl(2,0)\mathrm{Cl}(2,0). The implementation (Impl/Clifford.v) builds every such algebra at once: a blade is a subset of the generators, recorded as one bit per generator, and an element is one coefficient per blade —

Definition Cl (F : Type) (n : nat) : Type := Blade n -> F.

— with the multiplication fixed by a signature giving each generator’s square. The plane is the instance n=2n=2 at the signature (1,1)(1,1):

  Definition sig2 : Signature F 2 :=
    Vector.cons F 1 1 (Vector.cons F 1 0 (Vector.nil F)).

A blade with an even number of generators has even parity, and the even part of the algebra — here spanned by 1 and e12e_{12} alone, built from an even number of vector factors — is the elements supported on the even blades:

  Definition cl_even {n : nat} (x : Cl F n) : Prop :=
    forall D : Blade n, bparity D = true -> x D == 0.

(bparity D = true says the blade DD holds an odd number of generators; an even element carries 0 on every such blade.) That even part is closed under multiplication, and in the plane it turns out to match the complex numbers exactly.

The plane’s even part is the complex numbers

The four blades of the plane algebra are named by their two bits, and a concrete element is written by listing its four coefficients — scalar, e1e_1, e2e_2, e12e_{12}:

  Definition pb (b1 b2 : bool) : Blade 2 := bcons b1 (bcons b2 bnil).
  Definition mk2 (c a b p : F) : Cl F 2 :=
    fun D =>
      if bhead D
      then if bhead (btail D) then p else a
      else if bhead (btail D) then b else c.

The two directions of the correspondence with the complex numbers read off, or rebuild, the even part’s two surviving coordinates — the scalar blade pb false false and the bivector blade pb true true — against a complex number’s real and imaginary parts:

  Definition even_to_complex (x : Cl F 2) : C F :=
    mkC (x (pb false false)) (x (pb true true)).

  Definition complex_to_even (z : C F) : Cl F 2 := mk2 (re z) 0 0 (im z).

They are mutually inverse exactly on the even part. Round-tripping a general element of Cl F 2 through complex_to_even forgets its vector coordinates, so that direction only holds under cl_even; the other direction needs no hypothesis at all, because complex_to_even’s image is always even:

  Lemma even_round_trip : forall x : Cl F 2,
    cl_even x -> complex_to_even (even_to_complex x) == x.
  Lemma complex_round_trip : forall z : C F,
    even_to_complex (complex_to_even z) == z.

A bijection between two four- and two-dimensional carriers is cheap; what makes this a ring isomorphism rather than a relabeling is that multiplication matches too, and multiplication is where the two constructions could have disagreed. The plane algebra was built entirely from the relations e1e1=e2e2=1e_1e_1=e_2e_2=1, e1e2=−e2e1e_1e_2=-e_2e_1 — nothing in its definition mentions complex numbers. Checking that its multiplication, restricted to even elements, reproduces the complex numbers’ product is a real theorem, verified directly against the geometric product:

  Lemma even_to_complex_mul : forall x y : Cl F 2,
    cl_even x -> cl_even y ->
    even_to_complex (x * y) == even_to_complex x * even_to_complex y.

Contrast this with the smaller algebra Cl(0,1)\mathrm{Cl}(0,1): there, a one-dimensional space is embedded directly into the complex numbers, which are the carrier by definition — the match is built in from the start, a coincidence of carriers. Here Cl F 2 is an independent four-dimensional ring, built from the geometric relations alone, and its agreement with the complex numbers under multiplication had to be checked, and could in principle have failed.

Composing rotors is complex multiplication

A rotor is an even element, written directly by its two coordinates, with no angle mentioned:

  Definition rotor (c s : F) : Cl F 2 := mk2 c 0 0 s.

and reversal — the operation that will let a rotor act correctly on a vector — flips the sign of each blade’s coefficient according to how many transpositions it takes to reverse that blade’s generators, k(k−1)/2k(k-1)/2 for a grade-kk blade, defined once for every dimension:

  Definition cl_reverse {n : nat} (x : Cl F n) : Cl F n :=
    fun D => parity_sign (rev_parity (bcount D)) * x D.

At n=2n=2 this fixes the scalar and vector coordinates and negates exactly the bivector one — reversing the order of the two vectors inside e12=e1e2e_{12}=e_1e_2 gives e2e1=−e12e_2e_1=-e_{12}:

  Lemma reverse_mk2 : forall c a b p : F,
    cl_reverse (mk2 c a b p) == mk2 c a b (- p).

Composing two rotors — multiplying them as elements of Cl(2,0)\mathrm{Cl}(2,0) — gives back a rotor, at the ordinary angle-addition pair:

rotor(c1,s1)rotor(c2,s2)=rotor(c1c2−s1s2,c1s2+s1c2).\mathrm{rotor}(c_1,s_1)\,\mathrm{rotor}(c_2,s_2) \;=\; \mathrm{rotor}(c_1c_2-s_1s_2,\ c_1s_2+s_1c_2).

This is the cosine/sine addition formula without ever introducing an angle: c,sc, s are just field elements, constrained to c2+s2=1c^2+s^2=1 only once a rotor is required to preserve length.

Where does that formula come from? Not from the plane algebra: it is the multiplication table of any two-dimensional plane spanned by a ring’s unit and a square root of −1-1, and it can be multiplied out from the ring laws alone, with the square root’s defining equation as the only hypothesis:

  Theorem two_term_product : forall (a b c d : F) (e : A),
    e * e == - (1) ->
    (a *: 1 + b *: e) * (c *: 1 + d *: e)
    == (a * c + - (b * d)) *: 1 + (a * d + b * c) *: e.

To use that here, two things about the plane algebra have to be checked, and they are the two that really are about this representation: that a rotor is a point of the plane spanned by the unit and the bivector,

  Lemma rotor_two_term : forall c s : F,
    rotor c s == c *: (1 : Cl F 2) + s *: rotor 0 1.

and that the bivector squares to −1-1:

  Theorem plane_bivector_square :
    (rotor (0 : F) 1) * rotor 0 1 == - (1 : Cl F 2).
  Proof. apply cl2_ext; by_blade. Qed.

cl2_ext is the extensionality lemma of the plane algebra: two elements agreeing on the four blades are equal, so applying it turns the goal into four coefficient equations, one per blade of the result. by_blade then unfolds rotor, the geometric product, the unit and the negation over each concrete blade, leaving a polynomial identity in the coefficients, which ring discharges — the section declares Add Ring over the coefficient field, which is what makes the tactic available on a setoid equality rather than Coq’s built-in eq.

With those two in hand the rotor product is the general table read through the plane’s coordinates, and no blade appears in the argument:

  Lemma rotor_mul : forall c1 s1 c2 s2 : F,
    rotor c1 s1 * rotor c2 s2
    == rotor (c1*c2 + - (s1*s2)) (c1*s2 + s1*c2).
  Proof.
    intros c1 s1 c2 s2.
    calc ((rotor c1 s1 * rotor c2 s2) : Cl F 2)
      == (((c1 *: (1 : Cl F 2) + s1 *: rotor 0 1) * rotor c2 s2) : Cl F 2)
         { (rotor_two_term c1 s1) }
      == (((c1 *: (1 : Cl F 2) + s1 *: rotor 0 1)
             * (c2 *: (1 : Cl F 2) + s2 *: rotor 0 1)) : Cl F 2)
         { (rotor_two_term c2 s2) }
      == (((c1*c2 + - (s1*s2)) *: (1 : Cl F 2)
             + (c1*s2 + s1*c2) *: rotor 0 1) : Cl F 2)
         { (two_term_product c1 s1 c2 s2 (rotor 0 1) plane_bivector_square) }
      == ((rotor (c1*c2 + - (s1*s2)) (c1*s2 + s1*c2)) : Cl F 2)
         { (rotor_two_term (c1*c2 + - (s1*s2)) (c1*s2 + s1*c2)) }.
  Qed.

That this matches complex multiplication is not a second coincidence — it is one instance of the homomorphism theorem above, since a rotor is always even (rotor_even). Putting the two together — the image of a product of rotors equals the product of images, and the product of rotors is itself the angle-added rotor — gives the statement that composing rotors literally is multiplying complex numbers:

  Theorem rotor_composition_is_u1 : forall c1 s1 c2 s2 : F,
    even_to_complex (rotor c1 s1) * even_to_complex (rotor c2 s2)
    == even_to_complex (rotor (c1*c2 + - (s1*s2)) (c1*s2 + s1*c2)).
  Proof.
    intros c1 s1 c2 s2.
    calc (even_to_complex (rotor c1 s1) * even_to_complex (rotor c2 s2))
      == (even_to_complex (rotor c1 s1 * rotor c2 s2))
         { (even_to_complex_mul (rotor c1 s1) (rotor c2 s2)
              (rotor_is_even c1 s1) (rotor_is_even c2 s2)) }
      == (even_to_complex (rotor (c1*c2 + - (s1*s2)) (c1*s2 + s1*c2)))
         { rotor_mul }.
  Qed.

The calc chain has two steps, each justified in braces. The first turns the left side, a product of two complex images, back into the image of a single product of rotors (even_to_complex_mul, read right to left, with rotor_even supplying the two evenness hypotheses); the second replaces that product of rotors with the already-computed angle-added rotor (rotor_mul), landing on the right side exactly. The unit-length elements of the complex numbers form a group under multiplication by definition — that group is what “U(1)” names — and this theorem says that group’s operation is exactly what composing two rotors already does. Unit length itself is the condition c2+s2=1c^2+s^2=1, and it is precisely what makes a rotor invert by its own reversal (rotor_unit):

  Lemma rotor_unit : forall c s : F,
    c*c + s*s == 1 -> rotor c s * cl_reverse (rotor c s) == 1.

Space, spinors, and the double cover

Space needs one more generator, e3e_3, still squaring to 1 and anticommuting with the other two: the instance n=3n=3 at the signature (1,1,1)(1,1,1), an eight-dimensional ring — scalar, three vectors, three bivectors e12,e13,e23e_{12}, e_{13}, e_{23}, and one trivector e123e_{123}. A concrete element lists its eight coefficients:

  Definition mk3 (c a1 a2 a3 p12 p13 p23 t : F) : Cl F 3 :=
    fun D =>
      if bhead D
      then if bhead (btail D)
           then if bhead (btail (btail D)) then t   else p12
           else if bhead (btail (btail D)) then p13 else a1
      else if bhead (btail D)
           then if bhead (btail (btail D)) then p23 else a2
           else if bhead (btail (btail D)) then a3  else c.

The even part is four-dimensional — the scalar and the three bivectors — and its general element is called a spinor:

  Definition spinor (r b12 b13 b23 : F) : Cl F 3 :=
    mk3 r 0 0 0 b12 b13 b23 0.
  Lemma spinor_even : forall r b12 b13 b23 : F,
    cl_even (spinor r b12 b13 b23).

Just as in the plane, the even part is closed under the geometric product — the product of two even elements is even:

  Lemma even_mul_closed : forall x y : Cl F 3,
    cl_even x -> cl_even y -> cl_even (x * y).
  Proof. exact even_mul_even. Qed.

That one-line proof is worth reading twice. even_mul_even is a law, not a lemma about Cl F 3: a parity grading of a ring is an even part and an odd part, each closed under addition, whose products obey the ℤ/2\mathbb{Z}/2 rule — even times even is even, even times odd is odd, odd times odd is even again. The Clifford algebras satisfy it in every dimension and at every signature, for one reason that fits in a sentence: the product’s coefficient at a blade DD is a sum over blades AA of xAyA⊕Dx_A\,y_{A \oplus D}, and if xx vanishes off one parity and yy off another, then at any DD whose parity is not their sum every single term has a vanishing factor. So spinors multiply to spinors, and “orientation composed so far” never leaves the four even coordinates. (This four-dimensional even subalgebra is, classically, the quaternions — the same way the plane’s two-dimensional even part is the complex numbers; the identification is not re-proved here.)

A spinor acts on a vector the way the plane’s rotor will act below: by the sandwich

  Definition rotate (R x : Cl F 3) : Cl F 3 := R * x * cl_reverse R.

so ρR(x)=RxR̃\rho_R(x) = R\,x\,\tilde R with R̃\tilde R the reversal. When RR is even, the sandwich sends every vector to another vector — proved with no unit hypothesis RR̃=1R\tilde R = 1 anywhere in it:

  Lemma rotate_coordinates_correct : forall (R : Cl F 3) (v : Fin.t 3 -> F),
    cl_even R -> rotate R (ga_vec v) == ga_vec (rotate_coordinates R v).

(ga_vec v embeds the coordinate triple vv as a grade-1 element; rotate_coordinates R v names the three coefficients the sandwich produces.) For a spinor, those three coefficients are computed out in full:

  Lemma rotate_spinor_vec : forall r b12 b13 b23 v1 v2 v3 : F,
    rotate (spinor r b12 b13 b23) (ga_vec (space_vector v1 v2 v3))
    == ga_vec (space_vector
         ((r*r + - (b12*b12) + - (b13*b13) + b23*b23) * v1
          + (r*b12 + r*b12 + - (b13*b23 + b13*b23)) * v2
          + (r*b13 + r*b13 + b12*b23 + b12*b23) * v3)
         ((- (r*b12 + r*b12) + - (b13*b23 + b13*b23)) * v1
          + (r*r + - (b12*b12) + b13*b13 + - (b23*b23)) * v2
          + (r*b23 + r*b23 + - (b12*b13 + b12*b13)) * v3)
         ((b12*b23 + b12*b23 + - (r*b13 + r*b13)) * v1
          + (- (r*b23 + r*b23) + - (b12*b13 + b12*b13)) * v2
          + (r*r + b12*b12 + - (b13*b13) + - (b23*b23)) * v3)).

This statement is a 3×33\times 3 rotation matrix written out entry by entry, and one structural feature of it carries the fact promised in the opening section. Look at any entry: every summand is a product of exactly two of the spinor’s four coefficients — rrr\,r, rb12r\,b_{12}, b13b23b_{13}b_{23}, never a bare rr or a triple product. Now replace the spinor R=(r,b12,b13,b23)R = (r, b_{12}, b_{13}, b_{23}) by its negation −R=(−r,−b12,−b13,−b23)-R = (-r, -b_{12}, -b_{13}, -b_{23}): each summand picks up two minus signs, which cancel, so every entry of the matrix — and therefore the rotated vector itself — is unchanged:

ρ−R(v)=(−R)v(−R)̃=(−1)(−1)⋅RvR̃=ρR(v).\rho_{-R}(v) \;=\; (-R)\,v\,\widetilde{(-R)} \;=\; (-1)(-1)\cdot R\,v\,\tilde R \;=\; \rho_R(v).

(The middle step is the same cancellation stated abstractly: reversal only ever rescales coefficients, so it is linear, −R̃=−R̃\widetilde{-R} = -\tilde R, and the two minus signs meet and cancel.) A spinor and its negation act identically on every vector — that is what the quoted theorem shows on its face, once the substitution is made. This is the algebraic fact behind the physical claim that something built the way a spinor is built must be turned by 720°720°, not 360°360°, to return to where it started: a continuous family of spinors tracing out a full circle of actual rotations brings the rotations themselves back to “no rotation,” but need not bring the spinor itself back to where it began — it may instead land on the negation, which produces exactly the same rotations, indistinguishable by anything built out of vectors alone.

Getting the full Standard Model group SU(3)×SU(2)×U(1)SU(3)\times SU(2)\times U(1) out of one Clifford algebra is not settled the way the results above are settled: it is a small cluster of competing, mutually citing single-paper proposals — Trayling & Baylis (J. Phys. A 34 (2001) 3309) and Furey (Eur. Phys. J. C 78 (2018) 375) among them — none endorsed by mainstream particle physics, each reproducing known particle charges by construction rather than predicting anything new; the one genuinely uncontested piece of adjacent mathematics is that the automorphism group of the octonions is the exceptional Lie group G2G_2, and the subgroup of G2G_2 fixing one chosen unit imaginary octonion is exactly SU(3)SU(3) (Günaydin & Gürsey, J. Math. Phys. 14 (1973) 1651; canonical write-up: Baez, Bull. Amer. Math. Soc. 39 (2002) 145) — real, checkable, and a separate piece of mathematics from the theorems proved above, not another instance of the same argument.

Using it: a rotation matrix, with no trigonometry

Back in the plane, wrap a rotor around a vector the way rotate above wrapped a spinor around one of space’s:

  Definition sandwich (R x : Cl F 2) : Cl F 2 := R * x * cl_reverse R.

Feed it a pure vector — an element with only its e1,e2e_1, e_2 coordinates set, ga_vec (plane_vector v1 v2) where plane_vector a b is just the pair (a,b)(a,b) read as a function on two indices. Computed directly from the geometric product, with no hypothesis on c,sc,s anywhere:

  Lemma sandwich_rotor_vec : forall c s v1 v2 : F,
    sandwich (rotor c s) (ga_vec (plane_vector v1 v2))
    == ga_vec (plane_vector
         ((c*c + - (s*s)) * v1 + (c*s + c*s) * v2)
         (- ((c*s + c*s) * v1) + (c*c + - (s*s)) * v2)).

Wrapping rotor(c,s)\mathrm{rotor}(c,s) around (v1,v2)(v_1,v_2) returns (w1,w2)(w_1,w_2) with

w1=(c2−s2)v1+2csv2,w2=−2csv1+(c2−s2)v2,w_1 = (c^2-s^2)v_1 + 2cs\,v_2, \qquad w_2 = -2cs\,v_1 + (c^2-s^2)v_2,

the matrix

(c2−s22cs−2csc2−s2).\begin{pmatrix} c^2-s^2 & 2cs \\ -2cs & c^2-s^2\end{pmatrix}.

Once c2+s2=1c^2+s^2=1, these are exactly the double-angle formulas cos⁡2θ=c2−s2\cos 2\theta = c^2-s^2 and sin⁡2θ=2cs\sin 2\theta = 2cs for c=cos⁡θ,s=sin⁡θc=\cos\theta, s=\sin\theta — but nothing above ever invoked cos\cos, sin\sin, or a trigonometric identity; the whole computation is multiplying out the sandwich against the geometric product and regrouping terms. (The matrix is rotation by −2θ-2\theta in the usual counterclockwise-positive convention rather than +2θ+2\theta — a real consequence of writing the bivector as e1e2e_1e_2 rather than e2e1e_2e_1, not an error.) A further theorem checks that the squared length is exactly preserved — the sandwich is a genuine rotation, not merely some linear map that happens to look like one:

  Lemma rotation_preserves_qform : forall c s v1 v2 : F,
    c*c + s*s == 1 ->
    qform sig2 (plane_vector
        ((c*c + - (s*s)) * v1 + (c*s + c*s) * v2)
        (- ((c*s + c*s) * v1) + (c*c + - (s*s)) * v2))
    == qform sig2 (plane_vector v1 v2).

qform sig2 is the squared length the signature (1,1)(1,1) induces, v12+v22v_1^2+v_2^2 (qform_plane), so the statement reads: the rotated vector is exactly as long as the original. Six polynomials in four unknowns, and none of them is the reason it holds. The reason is one line of algebra available in any geometric algebra at all: a vector’s square is its quadratic value, so

(RvR∼)(RvR∼)=Rv(R∼R)vR∼=R(Q(v)1)R∼=Q(v)RR∼=Q(v)1,(RvR^{\sim})(RvR^{\sim}) = R\,v\,(R^{\sim}R)\,v\,R^{\sim} = R\,(Q(v)\,1)\,R^{\sim} = Q(v)\,R R^{\sim} = Q(v)\,1,

where the inner pair cancels because a unit rotor is inverted by its own reversal, and the scalar walks out through both sides because scalars are central. That is the theorem, and the plane’s version is an instance of it:

  Theorem sandwich_qform_scale : forall (R : A) (u w : V),
    R * rev R == 1 -> rev R * R == 1 ->
    ga_vec w == R * ga_vec u * rev R ->
    Q w *: (1 : A) == Q u *: 1.

One gap has to be closed to get from there to an equation between two scalars: the displayed argument ends with two multiples of the algebra’s unit being equal, and in a one-element algebra that says nothing at all. What is needed is that the scalar line is faithful —

Definition one_faithful (F A : Type) {Fe : EqRel F} {Ae : EqRel A}
    {Ao : One A} {As : HasScale F A} : Prop :=
  forall a b : F, a *: (1 : A) == b *: 1 -> a == b.

— and in the plane algebra that is one blade: read both sides at the scalar blade pb false false and the scalars are back (plane_one_faithful).
