Maxwell’s four equations, one equation

Electromagnetism is usually taught as four separate laws, each pinned to its own scrap of vector calculus. Gauss’s law for the electric field says its divergence equals the charge density, ∇⋅E=ρ\nabla\cdot E=\rho. The law of no magnetic monopoles says the magnetic field’s divergence is always zero, ∇⋅B=0\nabla\cdot B=0 — an extra postulate about what magnetic fields are allowed to look like, not a consequence of anything else. Faraday’s law of induction ties a changing magnetic field to a circulating electric field, ∇×E=−∂B/∂t\nabla\times E=-\partial B/\partial t. The Ampère–Maxwell law does the mirror thing for the magnetic field, tying it to an electric current and a changing electric field, ∇×B−∂E/∂t=j\nabla\times B-\partial E/\partial t=j. Four laws, four separate statements — two about divergence, two about curl — presented one after another, with nothing said about why there happen to be exactly four, or why they split between EE and BB the particular way they do.

That split has a historical excuse. Static electricity and permanent magnets were studied as separate subjects for a couple of centuries before Faraday found that a changing magnetic field pushes on electric charge, and Maxwell supplied the matching term making a changing electric field do the same to a magnetic field. Only at that point did EE and BB become one dynamical system, described by the four laws above.

Which raises the question the usual four-laws presentation never answers: is the four-ness a fundamental feature of electricity and magnetism, or a leftover of the historical accident that these were two separate subjects before they were one? Settling that precisely takes more than a suggestive remark. It takes one single algebraic object built out of EE and BB together — not a pair (E,B)(E,B) carried side by side, but one genuinely unified entity — and one single operator generalizing both divergence and curl at once, such that one equation applied to that one object reproduces exactly the four laws above, with nothing further assumed anywhere in the process.

The spacetime algebra behind E and B

A geometric algebra multiplies vectors, not just adds and scales them. The one rule governing that product is that a vector times itself is always a scalar, its own squared length; from that single rule, the product of two different vectors splits into a symmetric scalar part (their ordinary inner product) and an antisymmetric part called a bivector — not a number and not another vector, but a new algebraic object built from two directions at once, recording which plane they span and which way around it turns. Multiplying together more independent vectors keeps climbing: three give a trivector, and in an algebra built from nn basis vectors the ladder tops out at grade nn, one pseudoscalar spanning all nn directions together. That rung number — 00 for a scalar, 11 for a vector, 22 for a bivector, and so on — is the element’s grade.

Spacetime algebra (STA) is this same construction, built from four basis vectors instead of two or three: one timelike direction γ0\gamma_0 and three spacelike directions γ1,γ2,γ3\gamma_1,\gamma_2,\gamma_3. Whether γ0\gamma_0 squares to +1+1 and the spacelike ones to −1-1, or the other way around, is a labeling choice, called Cl(1,3)\mathrm{Cl}(1,3) or Cl(3,1)\mathrm{Cl}(3,1) respectively. This is uncontested textbook mathematics — Hestenes’ spacetime-algebra reformulation of electrodynamics (Space-Time Algebra, 1966; New Foundations for Classical Mechanics, 2003) and Doran & Lasenby’s textbook treatment (Geometric Algebra for Physicists, CUP 2003, ch. 5–7). The construction used here (Impl/Clifford.v) builds the Clifford algebra of any number of generators in one definition: a blade is a subset of the generators, recorded as one bit per generator, and an element of the algebra is one coefficient per blade:

Definition Cl (F : Type) (n : nat) : Type := Blade n -> F.

At n=4n=4 there are 24=162^4=16 blades — 1+4+6+4+11+4+6+4+1, grouped by grade: one scalar (the empty subset), four vectors, six bivectors, four trivectors, one pseudoscalar — so an element of Cl F 4 is exactly the sixteen coefficients a general STA multivector carries. Which ring those sixteen coefficients form is fixed by a signature, one square per generator:

Definition Signature (F : Type) (n : nat) : Type := Vector.t F n.
  Definition sig13 : Signature F 4 :=
    Vector.cons F (- (1)) 3 (Vector.cons F 1 2
      (Vector.cons F 1 1 (Vector.cons F 1 0 (Vector.nil F)))).

sig13 squares γ0\gamma_0 to −1-1 and the three spacelike generators to +1+1: the (−,+,+,+)(-,+,+,+) convention, the opposite of the (+,−,−,−)(+,-,-,-) that Hestenes and Doran & Lasenby write. The two conventions produce genuinely different rings, but every theorem below still states the textbook coefficients, because the file compensates by swapping the order of the one product it takes. Flipping the metric negates exactly the grade-1 coefficients of ∇F\nabla F — and so does swapping the two factors — so F∇F\nabla computed in (−,+,+,+)(-,+,+,+) agrees, blade for blade, with ∇F\nabla F computed in (+,−,−,−)(+,-,-,-). Every displayed statement below is therefore written F_field * Nabla in the code, and is read throughout as the textbook ∇F\nabla F.

That the signature really does govern the squares of vectors is one instance of the contraction law, proved once for every dimension and signature (gv_square) and instantiated at this one:

  Example cl13_contraction : forall v : Fin.t 4 -> F,
    cl_mul sig13 (ga_vec v) (ga_vec v) == qform sig13 v *: cl_one.
  Proof. exact (gv_square 4 sig13). Qed.

ga_vec v embeds a coordinate vector as a grade-1 element, cl_mul sig13 is the geometric product at this signature, cl_one is the algebra’s unit, and qform sig13 v is the quadratic form the signature induces, −v02+v12+v22+v32-v_0^2+v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2. Read at the four basis vectors in turn: γ02=−1\gamma_0^2=-1 and γ12=γ22=γ32=+1\gamma_1^2=\gamma_2^2=\gamma_3^2=+1, each sitting as a plain number on the unit.

Given the electric and magnetic field components at a spacetime point, E1,E2,E3E_1,E_2,E_3 and B1,B2,B3B_1,B_2,B_3, and the four spacetime derivatives there, d0,d1,d2,d3d_0,d_1,d_2,d_3 (standing for ∂t,∂x,∂y,∂z\partial_t,\partial_x, \partial_y,\partial_z), Theory/Maxwell.v discloses all ten as bare elements of the field FF:

  Context (E1 E2 E3 B1 B2 B3 : F).
  Context (d0 d1 d2 d3 : F).

Individual blades are named by their four bits, one per generator:

  Definition fb (b0 b1 b2 b3 : bool) : Blade 4 :=
    bcons b0 (bcons b1 (bcons b2 (bcons b3 bnil))).

so fb true true false false is the bivector blade γ0γ1\gamma_0\gamma_1, and applying an element of Cl F 4 to it reads off that element’s γ0γ1\gamma_0\gamma_1 coefficient. The field bivector packages the six field components into one element of grade 2, by matching on a blade’s four bits:

  Definition F_field : Cl F 4 :=
    fun D =>
      match bhead D, bhead (btail D), bhead (btail (btail D)),
            bhead (btail (btail (btail D))) with
      | true,  true,  false, false => E1
      | true,  false, true,  false => E2
      | true,  false, false, true  => E3
      | false, true,  true,  false => - B3
      | false, true,  false, true  => B2
      | false, false, true,  true  => - B1
      | _,     _,     _,     _     => 0
      end.

Reading off the six nonzero cases: E1,E2,E3E_1,E_2,E_3 sit on the blades {γ0,γ1},{γ0,γ2},{γ0,γ3}\{\gamma_0,\gamma_1\},\{\gamma_0,\gamma_2\},\{\gamma_0,\gamma_3\}, the three timelike bivectors, each mixing γ0\gamma_0 with one spacelike direction — the electric field is what a bivector looks like when one of its two directions is the observer’s own time axis. B1,B2,B3B_1,B_2,B_3 sit, as −B3,B2,−B1-B_3,B_2,-B_1, on {γ1,γ2},{γ1,γ3},{γ2,γ3}\{\gamma_1,\gamma_2\},\{\gamma_1,\gamma_3\}, \{\gamma_2,\gamma_3\}, the three purely spacelike bivectors — the standard construction F=E+IBF=E+IB, where I=γ1γ2γ3I=\gamma_1\gamma_2\gamma_3 is the purely spatial pseudoscalar and IBIB turns the magnetic field vector into its dual bivector. The particular signs −B3,B2,−B1-B_3,B_2,-B_1 were fixed by direct computation, not picked among several equally plausible options: flipping the sign of the BB part alone, leaving EE fixed, flips the relative sign between the two laws that fall out of the theorem below, and only this pattern reproduces the conventional ∇×E=−∂B/∂t\nabla\times E=-\partial B/\partial t (not +∂B/∂t+\partial B/\partial t) and ∇⋅B=0\nabla\cdot B=0.

The other half of the product is the vector derivative itself, ∇=γ0∂0+γ1∂1+γ2∂2+γ3∂3\nabla=\gamma_0\partial_0+\gamma_1\partial_1+\gamma_2\partial_2 +\gamma_3\partial_3, one partial derivative per spacetime direction, packaged as a single grade-1 vector:

  Definition spacetime_vector (v0 v1 v2 v3 : F) : Fin.t 4 -> F :=
    fun i => match i with
             | Fin.F1 => v0
             | Fin.FS Fin.F1 => v1
             | Fin.FS (Fin.FS Fin.F1) => v2
             | Fin.FS (Fin.FS (Fin.FS _)) => v3
             end.

  Definition Nabla : Cl F 4 := ga_vec (spacetime_vector d0 d1 d2 d3).

Building an actual differential operator would need real multivariable calculus: functions of a spacetime point, and a genuine notion of differentiation applied to them (Theory/Derivative.v’s is_deriv is the tool that would eventually supply it). This file does not build that — it takes the same step Theory/Log.v takes with its exp_surj hypothesis and Theory/Action.v with ftc: rather than construct the real analytic object, it states exactly the algebraic fact needed, as an explicit hypothesis, and proves what follows from that fact alone. Here the hypothesis is d0,…,d3d_0,\dots,d_3 standing for the four partial derivatives evaluated at one fixed point — reading “did_i times a field coefficient” as “the partial derivative of that field coefficient” is what turns the algebra below into the calculus identity it stands for, once a real derivative is substituted for the did_i. What is proved is the algebraic identity, not the calculus underneath it: the theorem holds for whatever derivative eventually fills that hypothesis, not only for one particular construction of it.

Why only a vector and a trivector can survive

Take any Clifford algebra, any dimension, any signature, and multiply a vector (grade 1) by a bivector (grade 2). The general rule for how the geometric product distributes across grades says a grade-rr element times a grade-ss element lands only on grades |r−s|,|r−s|+2,…,r+s|r-s|,|r-s|+2,\dots, r+s. For r=1,s=2r=1,s=2 that list is just {1,3}\{1,3\}: grade 00, grade 22, and grade 44 are not merely small, they are exactly absent — for every vector and every bivector, in every Clifford algebra whatsoever, before any physical field is plugged in. It is easy to expect a leftover scalar or a leftover bivector part and be wrong. What survives is a grade-1 part, conventionally written ∇⋅F\nabla\cdot F, and a grade-3 part, ∇∧F\nabla\wedge F — “dot” and “wedge” by analogy with the ordinary dot and cross products, though neither is symmetric or antisymmetric the way those are:

∇F=⟨∇F⟩1+⟨∇F⟩3=∇⋅F+∇∧F,⟨∇F⟩0=⟨∇F⟩2=⟨∇F⟩4=0.\nabla F \;=\; \langle\nabla F\rangle_1+\langle\nabla F\rangle_3 \;=\; \nabla\cdot F+\nabla\wedge F, \qquad \langle\nabla F\rangle_0=\langle\nabla F\rangle_2=\langle\nabla F\rangle_4=0.

Here is that fact, proved for this specific ∇\nabla and FF — eight statements, one per blade of grade 00, 22, or 44, each reading one coefficient of the product by applying it to that blade:

  Theorem F_nabla_grade_decomposition :
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false false false false) == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true true false false)   == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true false true false)   == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true false false true)   == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false true true false)   == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false true false true)   == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false false true true)   == 0 /\
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true true true true)     == 0.
  Proof. repeat split; by_blade. Qed.

repeat split breaks the eight-way conjunction into eight separate goals. by_blade is the file’s one reduction step, an Ltac abbreviation: it unfolds Nabla, F_field, and the geometric product itself over the one concrete blade in the goal — each coefficient of a product in Cl F 4 is by definition a signed sum over the sixteen blades — simplifies, and hands ring the field identity that remains. Each of the eight identities is a sum of products in which every term has a genuine zero factor — either from Nabla, which has no bivector, trivector, or pseudoscalar component to contribute, or from F_field, which has no scalar, vector, trivector, or pseudoscalar component. ring is Coq’s decision procedure for equations that hold in every commutative ring — it works here on the abstract field F itself, rather than a concrete number system, because a section-local Add Ring line registers it from nothing more than commutativity of multiplication, a separate mechanism not repeated here. Once the two operands are written out as explicit sixteen-coefficient sums, computing their product and reading off one output coefficient is bilinear expansion, exactly what ring normalizes — nothing about the electromagnetic field is used yet, only the shape of the multiplication.

Reading the four laws off ∇F

The four-current JJ — charge density and the three current-density components, packaged the same way ∇\nabla packages the four derivatives — is a single spacetime vector, grade 1, with no trivector part at all. That is what turns the theorem above into an answer to the question this post opened with. ∇F=J\nabla F=J is one equation; since ∇F\nabla F splits into a vector part and a trivector part, and JJ contributes nothing to the trivector slot, the one equation can only hold if the vector part of ∇F\nabla F equals JJ and, independently, the trivector part of ∇F\nabla F vanishes outright. Two conditions from one equation, because the object being set equal to ∇F\nabla F happens to occupy only one of the two grades ∇F\nabla F can ever touch.

The surviving vector part is Gauss’s law and the Ampère–Maxwell law. (The file works in natural units, c=1c=1 and ε0=μ0=1\varepsilon_0=\mu_0=1, so no constants decorate either law below.)

  Theorem F_nabla_0 :
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true false false false)
    == d1 * E1 + (d2 * E2 + d3 * E3).
  Proof. by_blade. Qed.

  Theorem F_nabla_1 :
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false true false false)
    == d0 * E1 + (- (d2 * B3) + d3 * B2).
  Proof. by_blade. Qed.

Reading d1,d2,d3d_1,d_2,d_3 as ∂x,∂y,∂z\partial_x,\partial_y,\partial_z and d0d_0 as ∂t\partial_t: F_nabla_0 reads the γ0\gamma_0 coefficient and states ∂xE1+∂yE2+∂zE3=∇⋅E\partial_xE_1+\partial_yE_2+\partial_zE_3=\nabla\cdot E, matched against a charge density ρ\rho — Gauss’s law, ∇⋅E=ρ\nabla\cdot E=\rho. F_nabla_1 reads the γ1\gamma_1 coefficient and states ∂tE1−(∂yB3−∂zB2)=∂tE1−(∇×B)1\partial_tE_1- (\partial_yB_3-\partial_zB_2)=\partial_tE_1-(\nabla\times B)_1, the xx-component of the Ampère–Maxwell law, matched against a current density component as ⟨∇F⟩γ1=−j1\langle\nabla F\rangle_{\gamma_1}=-j_1 (a relative sign between charge and current density that this signature attaches to spacelike components — the same feature behind Doran & Lasenby’s own J=ργ0+jkγkJ=\rho\gamma_0+j^k\gamma_k, not a mistake here).

The surviving trivector part is no magnetic monopoles and Faraday’s law:

  Theorem F_nabla_123 :
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false true true true)
    == - (d1 * B1) + (- (d2 * B2) + - (d3 * B3)).
  Proof. by_blade. Qed.
  Theorem F_nabla_012 :
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true true true false)
    == - (d0 * B3) + (- (d1 * E2) + d2 * E1).
  Proof. by_blade. Qed.

F_nabla_123 states that the γ1γ2γ3\gamma_1\gamma_2\gamma_3 coefficient is −(∂xB1+∂yB2+∂zB3)=−∇⋅B-(\partial_xB_1+\partial_yB_2+\partial_zB_3)=-\nabla\cdot B; F_nabla_012 states that the γ0γ1γ2\gamma_0\gamma_1\gamma_2 coefficient is −∂tB3−(∂xE2−∂yE1)=−(∂tB3+(∇×E)3)-\partial_tB_3-(\partial_xE_2-\partial_yE_1)=-\big(\partial_tB_3+ (\nabla\times E)_3\big), the zz-component of ∇×E+∂tB\nabla\times E+\partial_tB. Both are unconditional identities, proved exactly like the vector-part theorems, purely by ring — identities for what these two coefficients equal, not for their vanishing. Nothing proved so far forces either coefficient to be zero: the grade-decomposition theorem only reaches the scalar, bivector, and pseudoscalar coefficients; the trivector part is exactly the part that theorem leaves alone.

That vanishing — ∇⋅B=0\nabla\cdot B=0 and ∇×E=−∂tB\nabla\times E=-\partial_tB, the no-monopoles law and Faraday’s law — enters the final theorem as an explicit hypothesis, not a conclusion:

  Definition J : Cl F 4 :=
    ga_vec (spacetime_vector
      (d1 * E1 + (d2 * E2 + d3 * E3))
      (d0 * E1 + (- (d2 * B3) + d3 * B2))
      (d0 * E2 + (d1 * B3 + - (d3 * B1)))
      (d0 * E3 + (- (d1 * B2) + d2 * B1))).

  Theorem F_nabla_eq_J :
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb false true true true) == 0 ->
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true true true false) == 0 ->
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true true false true) == 0 ->
    (F_field * Nabla) (fb true false true true) == 0 ->
    F_field * Nabla == J.

JJ’s four components are read directly off F_nabla_0..F_nabla_3 — that is what “the vector part of ∇F\nabla F” means. The four premises state that all four trivector coefficients vanish: no monopoles and Faraday’s law, component by component. The theorem does not derive these four facts from F_field, Nabla, or the grade-decomposition theorem — it assumes them, because they are not algebraic consequences of the field bivector’s shape alone. Classically, a field bivector built from a four-potential AA via F=∇∧AF=\nabla\wedge A satisfies them automatically, since the trivector part of ∇F\nabla F then reduces to ∇∧∇∧A\nabla\wedge\nabla\wedge A, which vanishes for any AA by an identity about repeated derivatives. This file does not build a four-potential or that identity, so the four hypotheses stand as stated, doing the work that construction would otherwise supply for free.
