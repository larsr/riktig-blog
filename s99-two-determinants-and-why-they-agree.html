Two determinants, and why they must agree

There are several ways to compute a determinant. Expand along a column in minors and cofactors. Sum over all permutations with signs. Row-reduce and multiply the pivots. Or, in a geometric algebra, multiply the columns together and read off one coefficient.

Write two of those down as programs and you have two functions. Run them on the same matrix and they agree. Run them on a thousand matrices and they still agree. That is evidence, not a reason — and if you want the reason, testing more matrices will never supply it.

The reason has to come from somewhere else: from a description of what a determinant is that neither program mentions, and that both can be shown to satisfy.

What the determinant is

Three properties pin it down. It takes n vectors of an n-dimensional space and returns a scalar; it is multilinear — linear in each argument with the others held fixed; it is alternating — zero whenever two of its arguments are equal; and it is normalised — it returns 1 on the basis.

No formula there, and no matrix. In Coq that is three fields:

Class Determinant (n : nat) (I F V : Type)
    {eI : EqRel I} {Ieq : EquDec I} {Vu : HasUpd I V} {IFin : FinIndex n I}
    {Fe : EqRel F} {Fz : Zero F} {Fa : HasAdd F} {Fn : HasNeg F} {Fo : One F}
    {Fm : HasMult F} {Fi : HasInv F} {FF : Field F}
    {eV : EqRel V} {zV : Zero V} {aV : HasAdd V} {nV : HasNeg V} {sV : HasScale F V}
    {VV : VectorSpace F V} {Vl : HasLincomb I F V} (B : Basis n I F V) := {
  determinant_form : MultilinearForm I F V;
  determinant_is_alternating :: Alternating determinant_form;
  determinant_normalization : determinant_form (Basis.vec B) == 1;
}.

The long list in braces is the ambient equipment — that the index is finite and has a decidable equality, that the scalars form a field, that the vectors form a vector space over them. Coq fills those in by search at each use. The three lines after := { are the content, and they are the three properties above.

A class in Coq declares a family of facts indexed by types, and an instance registers one such fact in a database the proof search consults. So Determinant n I F V B is the claim “here is a form on V with these three properties”, and an instance is something that supplies one.

But read that sentence again: here is a form. Nothing yet says there is only one. Until that is proved, the class is a property that things may happen to have, and two things having it are under no obligation to agree. The whole plan rests on a theorem that was, until recently, not in the library at all.

The top exterior power is one-dimensional

The theorem says any two alternating multilinear n-forms are proportional. The usual statement introduces a scalar cc with u=cwu = c\,w, which forces you to worry about ww being zero. Cross-multiplying avoids that entirely: for a basis ee, and any family of vectors vv,

u(v)⋅w(e)=w(v)⋅u(e).u(v)\cdot w(e) \;=\; w(v)\cdot u(e).

No scalar to invert, no non-vanishing hypothesis. In Coq:

  Theorem alternating_forms_proportional : forall (u w : MultilinearForm I F V),
    Alternating u -> Alternating w ->
    forall vs : I -> V, Proper (equ ==> equ) vs ->
      u vs * w (Basis.vec B) == w vs * u (Basis.vec B).

The Proper (equ ==> equ) vs says the family of vectors respects the index’s own notion of equality — it does not hand back different vectors for two indices the index type considers the same. It is free whenever that equality is ordinary equality.

Uniqueness is now three lines of arithmetic. Set u=Du = D and w=D′w = D', two determinants over the same basis. Both normalisations are 1, so the two products collapse:

D(v)=D(v)⋅D′(e)=D′(v)⋅D(e)=D′(v)D(v) \;=\; D(v)\cdot D'(e) \;=\; D'(v)\cdot D(e) \;=\; D'(v)

and in Coq that is exactly what the proof says, one step per equality:

  Theorem determinant_unique : forall (D D' : Determinant n I F V B)
      (vs : I -> V), Proper (equ ==> equ) vs -> D vs == D' vs.
  Proof.
    intros D D' vs Pvs.
    calc (D vs)
      == (D vs * D' (Basis.vec B))
      == (D' vs * D (Basis.vec B))
      == (D' vs).
    - rewrite (determinant_basis_normalization D'). ring.
    - exact (alternating_forms_proportional B (determinant_multilinear_form D)
               (determinant_multilinear_form D') (determinant_alternating D)
               (determinant_alternating D') vs Pvs).
    - rewrite (determinant_basis_normalization D). ring.
  Qed.

The first and third steps multiply by a normalisation that is 1; the middle step is the proportionality theorem. calc chains equalities and discharges them by transitivity, one justification per step.

With that, the class stops being a property and becomes a definition. “The determinant” now names a single function, and anything proved to have the three properties is it.

The second implementation

In a geometric algebra, vectors can be multiplied. The product of n vectors decomposes into pieces of different grades, and the top piece — the one spanning the whole space — is what the wedge product would give. Reading off its coefficient turns n vectors into a scalar.

That is a determinant, and the library proves so over an abstract algebra: given a linear functional top that vanishes on everything below the top grade, the map sending a family of vectors to top (their product) is multilinear, alternating and normalised. No dimension, no coordinates, no matrix appears in that theorem.

At a concrete Clifford algebra it becomes a program:

  Definition det_ga (A : Matrix n n F) : F :=
    cl_top (cl_product s (map (fun j i => A i j) (fin_all n))).

Take the matrix’s columns, embed each as a vector, multiply them together, read the coefficient at the full blade. The fold runs over fin_all n, the index type’s own enumeration, so no bound and no index arithmetic appears — and there are no minors, no signs and no permutations anywhere in it.

It computes:

Example det_ga_3x3 : det_ga (sig_euclid 3) m3 == (-3)#1.
Proof. vm_compute. reflexivity. Qed.

for m3 the matrix with rows 1 2 3, 4 5 6, 7 8 10.

Why they agree

The library’s other determinant is a recursive cofactor expansion, and it too is proved to satisfy the three properties. So now there are two instances of one class — and uniqueness applies:

  Theorem det_ga_is_detM : forall A : Matrix n n F,
    det_ga A == detM (fun j i => A i j).
  Proof.
    intros A.
    exact (detM_is_the_determinant n det_ga_Determinant (fun j i => A i j)).
  Qed.

One line, and no induction on matrices, no comparison of the two algorithms, no shared code between them — one multiplies multivectors, the other recurses on minors. The transposition in the statement is bookkeeping: det_ga reads a matrix by its columns and the cofactor expansion reads the family as rows.

This is the pattern worth taking away. Two programs agree not because anyone checked them against each other, but because each was separately shown to meet a specification that admits only one answer. Adding a third — row reduction, say — costs one proof of the three properties, and its agreement with the other two is immediate.

One consequence fell out that was not designed for: det_ga is a determinant for every signature of the Clifford algebra, not only the Euclidean one. What the generators square to only shifts weight into grades below the top, and the functional does not look there.
