What a calculational proof compiles to: transitivity plus one law per step

A chain of expressions, glued by transitivity

There is a style of proof every algebra student learns at the blackboard: a column of expressions, each obtained from the one above it by one cited law, with the conclusion read off the two ends. Here is one, in a system with an addition, a zero, and a negation:

(a+b)+(−b)=a+(b+(−b))(associativity)=a+0(inverse)=a(identity) \begin{aligned} (a + b) + (-b) &= a + (b + (-b)) && \text{(associativity)} \\ &= a + 0 && \text{(inverse)} \\ &= a && \text{(identity)} \end{aligned}

Each adjacent pair of lines is one step, justified by one law. The steps are glued into a single claim about the two ends, (a+b)+(−b)=a(a+b)+(-b)=a, by transitivity: from x=yx=y and y=zy=z, conclude x=zx=z — nn lines is n−1n-1 uses of transitivity, so familiar the gluing is invisible.

Two things go unchecked at the blackboard: what “==” means — distinct representations of a value can be equal without being identical, as 12\tfrac12 and 24\tfrac24 are different pairs of integers but the same rational number, so a chain of “equalities” between representations is really a chain in some coarser relation, glued validly only because that relation is itself transitive — and what checking the chain requires: that each step follows from its cited law, that the endpoints really are the theorem’s two sides, and that the gluing uses an actual transitivity proof for the relation in play.

This post shows exactly what gets checked, for the chain notation used throughout this series: a chain is an ordinary piece of notation, and what it expands to is a sequence of transitivity calls plus one proof obligation per step.

Choosing what “equal” means

Coq’s built-in = is syntactic: two expressions are = when they compute to identical values, too fine for representations. Coq’s rationals are pairs — numerator and denominator, 1 # 2 for 12\tfrac12 — and 1 # 2 and 2 # 4 are different pairs, so 1 # 2 = 2 # 4 does not hold. The remedy is to pick, for each type, the relation that deserves to be called its equality: rationals use cross-multiplication (Qeq, notation ==), under which the two representations of one half are equal.

A type paired with a chosen equivalence relation is a setoid. What licenses a relation to be chained like equality is exactly the three properties of an equivalence relation — reflexivity, symmetry, and transitivity, the last one licensing the gluing.

A monoid is a carrier type with an addition, a zero, an equality, and three laws: associativity and the two identity laws. Those laws are spread over three small classes, each holding the previous one as a field: a Magma fixes the equality and demands that the operation respect it, a Semigroup adds associativity, a Monoid adds the two identities. Here are the first and the last, from Interface/Magma.v and Interface/Monoid.v:

Module Magma.
  Class Magma (M : Type) {eq : EqRel M} (op : BinOp M) := {
    equivalence :: Equivalence eq;
    op_proper   :: Proper (equ ==> equ ==> equ) op;
  }.
  Hint Mode Magma ! - - : typeclass_instances.
End Magma.
Export Magma.
Module Monoid.
  Class Monoid (M : Type) {eq : EqRel M} {zero : Zero M} {add : HasAdd M} := {
    semigroup         :: Semigroup M add;
    is_left_identity  :: LeftIdentity M M add 0;
    is_right_identity :: RightIdentity M M add 0;
  }.
  Hint Mode Monoid ! - - - : typeclass_instances.
  Hint Rewrite @left_identity @right_identity : algebra_db.
End Monoid.
Export Monoid.

The carrier’s equality, zero, and addition are class parameters (the {eq : EqRel M} binders); the fields are laws and substructures only. Two of the magma’s matter here. Magma.equivalence says the chosen == is an equivalence relation, so nothing in this tower can be declared over a relation that isn’t one. Magma.op_proper says the operation respects ==: the type Proper (equ ==> equ ==> equ) op is Coq’s spelling of “equal arguments give equal results,” and it is what lets a law rewrite an expression buried inside a larger one, such as replacing g + (-g) by 0 under an outer h + _.

Coq puts both to work without being asked. A typeclass declares a family of facts indexed by a type (or a relation); an instance registers one such fact in a database that Coq searches whenever a proof step needs a fact of that family — nothing is ever addressed by name. The :: in front of a field’s type is what registers it: writing equivalence :: Equivalence eq declares the field and files it as an instance in one stroke, so every magma makes Equivalence findable for its ==, and the standard library derives Transitive from Equivalence automatically. Monoid.semigroup is a :: field too, and so is the semigroup’s own magma field, so a monoid in scope puts all of it within one projection’s reach. That is why transitivity y — which splits a goal R x z into R x y and R y z — already finds its proof unassisted: it searches the database for Transitive at the relation in the goal.

The same search resolves each step’s cited law. right_identity, used below, is the method of a class in Props.v shaped x · unit == x for a given operation and unit, and Monoid.is_right_identity is an instance of that class — declared by its own ::, with no separate registration anywhere — so rewrite right_identity finds whichever instance matches the operation and unit in the goal.

The chain notation, in full

The notation lives in one file; its two working parts are each a few lines of Ltac, Coq’s language for proof-script procedures. The first checks the chain’s endpoints against the goal:

Ltac calc_ends x y :=
  lazymatch goal with
  | |- _ ?l ?r => unify l x; unify r y
  end.

|- _ ?l ?r matches a goal that is some relation applied to two arguments; unify demands that the written first and last terms of the chain equal the goal’s own two sides — a chain whose ends drifted from the theorem fails on its first token.

The second discharges one step:

Ltac calc_by r :=
  first [ now rewrite r
        | now rewrite <- r
        | now apply r
        | now (symmetry; apply r)
        | now exact r
        | now (symmetry; exact r) ].

first [...] tries each alternative and keeps the one that closes the goal completely (now): rewrite the law forward, rewrite it backward, apply it, apply it symmetrically, or use it as the exact proof term in either direction — a chain never states a direction because whichever alternative works is however the step is justified.

Everything else is surface syntax, one declaration per chain length. Here is the three-step form, in full:

Tactic Notation "calc" constr(x0)
    "==" constr(x1) "{" uconstr(r1) "}"
    "==" constr(x2) "{" uconstr(r2) "}"
    "==" constr(x3) "{" uconstr(r3) "}" :=
  calc_ends x0 x3;
  transitivity x1; [ calc_by r1 |
  transitivity x2; [ calc_by r2 | calc_by r3 ] ].

Read the body in order: check the endpoints against the goal, split at the first midpoint, close that step with its law, split the rest at the second midpoint, close the two remaining steps. That is the entire semantics of a chain — nn terms compile to n−1n-1 uses of transitivity and calc_by apiece. The file repeats the identical pattern up to eight terms: no recursion, nothing clever, one declaration per length.

A real group theorem, blackboard and Coq

Here is a theorem from Theory/Group.v: in any group, an element that cancels gg on the left is the inverse of gg — if h+g=0h + g = 0 then h=−gh = -g.

h=h+0(right identity)=h+(g+(−g))(right inverse)=(h+g)+(−g)(associativity)=0+(−g)(the hypothesis h+g=0)=−g(left identity) \begin{aligned} h &= h + 0 && \text{(right identity)} \\ &= h + (g + (-g)) && \text{(right inverse)} \\ &= (h + g) + (-g) && \text{(associativity)} \\ &= 0 + (-g) && \text{(the hypothesis } h + g = 0\text{)} \\ &= -g && \text{(left identity)} \end{aligned} 

  Theorem neg_unique_l : forall g h : G, h + g == 0 -> h == - g.
  Proof.
    intros g h Heq.
    calc h
      == (h + 0)          { right_identity  }
      == (h + (g + - g))  { right_inverse }
      == ((h + g) + - g)  { associative }
      == (0 + - g)        { Heq  }
      == (- g)            { left_identity  }.
  Qed.

intros brings in the group elements and Heq : h + g == 0. calc checks h and (- g) against the goal’s sides, then closes each step with calc_by on its cited law. right_identity erases the + 0. right_inverse collapses g + - g to 0, applied backward — the blackboard introduces the pair, calc_by finds the same law reads the other way. associative reassociates. Heq replaces h + g by 0, a brace citing a local hypothesis exactly like a registered law. left_identity erases the leading 0 +.

This six-term chain compiles the same way, one nesting level deeper: five transitivity splits and five calc_by calls, an ordinary Coq proof term that Qed accepts — the chain notation only spells it more legibly.

Writing a chain, and where else it applies

neg_unique_l used the justified form: one law per step, in braces, direction found automatically. Two siblings share the skeleton. The plain form drops the braces and proves each step afterward as its own bullet, in order — for a step needing more than a one-law rewrite. The named form gives each step a bracketed name instead, calc x ==[unit] y ==[cancel] z, proved afterward with Coq’s [name]: selector in any order. All three compile to the same transitivity/calc_by skeleton; only the bookkeeping around each step differs, so one chain may also mix the justified and named forms — one step named, the rest braced — which is how a single awkward step avoids promoting a whole chain to the named form. A variant spelled calcrw proves a small chain about a sub-term and then rewrites the goal with the result, so the unchanging context around that sub-term is written once, in the goal, instead of once per step.

The chain’s == is literal notation belonging to calc, not the setoid == of the monoid above: calc_ends reads whatever relation the goal is built from, and transitivity looks up a Transitive instance for that relation. So the same chains prove goals about Coq’s =, about the tower’s ==, and about relations like list permutation, unchanged. The rationals are the concrete case this post opened with — 1#1 and 2#2 are different pairs, equal only under Qeq, and that equality is decidable:

Example q_dec_eq_check : {(1#1)%Q == (2#2)%Q} + {(1#1)%Q =/= (2#2)%Q} :=
  dec_eq (1#1)%Q (2#2)%Q.
