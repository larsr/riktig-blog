The Jacobian, and the chain rule as matrix multiplication

The derivative post ended coordinate-free: the derivative of ff at aa is a linear map, and its composition rule is the chain rule. This post takes the last step into multivariable calculus by choosing bases and writing that map down as a table of numbers. The Jacobian turns out to require no new analysis at all — it is the linear-algebra machinery applied to an object the analysis already produced.

Writing a linear map as a matrix

Given a basis of the source and a basis of the target, any linear map has a matrix: its jj-th column is the image of the jj-th source basis vector, expressed in the target basis.

Aij=(coordinates of L(ej))iA_{ij} = \big(\text{coordinates of } L(e_j)\big)_i

  Definition mat_of (f : LinearMap F U V) : Matrix m n F :=
    fun i j => basis_coords BV (f (basis_vec BU j)) i.

That definition belongs to the linear-algebra part of the library and knows nothing about limits. The Jacobian is that construction applied to a derivative, and the definition is one line:

  Definition jacobian {f : U -> V} {a : U} {L : LinearMap F U V}
      (Hd : is_deriv f a L) : Matrix m n F :=
    mat_of BU BV L.

Notice the argument. jacobian takes not the function but a proof that L is its derivative at a. That is what makes it well defined: the matrix depends on the derivative map, and there is only one such map, by the uniqueness theorem in the previous post. A reader who wants the Jacobian must first have established that the function is differentiable — the type will not let them skip it.

The entries are the partial derivatives

The familiar description of the Jacobian is “the matrix of partial derivatives”. Here that is a theorem, and its proof is reflexivity:

  Theorem jacobian_col : forall {f : U -> V} {a : U} {L : LinearMap F U V}
      (Hd : is_deriv f a L) (i : Fin.t m) (j : Fin.t n),
    jacobian Hd i j == basis_coords BV (partial_at L BU j) i.
  Proof.
    intros f a L Hd i j.
    reflexivity.
  Qed.

Both sides unfold to the same term. mat_of builds its jj-th column by applying the map to the jj-th basis vector; partial_at is the map applied to the jj-th basis vector. The two definitions were made independently, in different files, and they coincide by construction — which is the sense in which the Jacobian is not a new idea but a re-reading of one.

For a function into the scalars, the matrix has a single row, and that row is the gradient: the same object, under the name it has when there is only one output to differentiate.

The chain rule becomes matrix multiplication

Now the theorem this was all for. The derivative post proved that the derivative of a composite is the composite of the derivatives. Choose bases for all three spaces and that statement becomes the rule every student of several-variable calculus learns:

  Theorem jacobian_chain_rule : forall (f : U -> V) (g : V -> W) (a : U)
      (Lf : LinearMap F U V) (Lg : LinearMap F V W)
      (Pg : Proper (equ ==> equ) g)
      (Hf : is_deriv f a Lf) (Hg : is_deriv g (f a) Lg),
    jacobian n p BU BW (deriv_comp f g a Lf Lg Pg Hf Hg)
      == mat_mul (jacobian m p BV BW Hg) (jacobian n m BU BV Hf).
  Proof.
    intros f g a Lf Lg Pg Hf Hg.
    apply mat_of_comp.
  Qed.

The proof is one line, and it is worth being clear about why, because it is the whole argument of this post. mat_of_comp is a fact of linear algebra: the matrix of a composition is the product of the matrices. deriv_comp is a fact of analysis: the derivative of a composition is the composition of the derivatives. The Jacobian chain rule is the first applied to the second, and needs nothing else. All the ε\varepsilons were spent in deriv_comp; none are spent again here.

There is no calc chain because there is nothing to chain: the goal is an instance of an existing theorem, and apply matches it, recovering the three bases and both maps by unification. A chain would be one step long and say less.

This also explains why matrix multiplication has the shape it does. It is not an arbitrary rule about rows and columns — it is what composition of linear maps looks like in coordinates, and the chain rule is where that shows most clearly.

Numbers, at last

the map u↦Afuu \mapsto A_f u is linear, hence its own derivative, and its partial derivatives are the entries read off in the right order:
