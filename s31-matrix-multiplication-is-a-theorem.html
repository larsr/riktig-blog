Matrix multiplication is not a definition

Everyone learns how to multiply two matrices before being told why that is the rule. You run along a row of the left grid and down a column of the right one, multiply the pairs you meet, and add them up:

(AB)ik=∑jAijBjk.(AB)_{ik} \;=\; \sum_j A_{ij}\, B_{jk}.

Adding two grids entry by entry is what anyone would guess. Doing that to multiply them is not, and “because it works” is not a reason.

The rule does have a source, and the source is not about grids. A linear map is a function between vector spaces that respects addition and scaling; it exists before anyone writes down a number. Choosing a basis for the source space and one for the target lets you write a map down: each source basis vector has an image, that image has coordinates in the target basis, and stacking those coordinate columns side by side gives a grid. Composing two maps, meanwhile, is ordinary composition of functions and involves no grids at all.

That leaves a question with one right answer. Which operation on grids makes the two agree — write down, then combine, versus compose, then write down? Answer it and the row-times-column rule stops being a convention to accept and becomes the only thing it could have been. What follows derives it, and then checks the derivation in Coq.

A grid of scalars costs nothing

A matrix is a function of two bounded indices — Fin.t m is the type with exactly m elements — into a field (Impl/Matrix.v):

Definition Matrix (m n : nat) (F : Type) : Type :=
  Fin.t m -> Fin.t n -> F.

Functions into a vector space form a vector space under pointwise operations. Reading the definition above as functions into functions into the field, and the field as a one-dimensional space over itself, that construction applies twice and gives matrices their zero, their addition and their scalar multiplication for free. The file records the fact as a check that Coq’s instance search assembles the structure unaided, with nothing to prove:

Example matrix_vs_check (F : Type) `{FF : Field F} (m n : nat) :
  VectorSpace F (Matrix m n F) := _.

Multiplication is the familiar sum, written with bigopF, the finite sum of a family indexed by Fin.t n:

Definition mat_mul {m n p : nat} (A : Matrix m n F) (B : Matrix n p F)
    : Matrix m p F :=
  fun i k => bigopF (fun j => A i j * B j k).

Its laws are all finite-sum bookkeeping. mat_mul_assoc is one application of bigopF_swap, the interchange ∑i∑j=∑j∑i\sum_i \sum_j = \sum_j \sum_i for finite sums, proved by induction in Theory/BigOp.v; the unit laws mat_mul_left_identity and mat_mul_right_identity are the standard-basis delta collapse, since mat_id is defined to be that delta. Square matrices therefore form a ring, Matrix_Ring — noncommutative, exactly as the ring of linear maps from a space to itself is. That the two rings match is not a coincidence, and the next two sections are why.

Writing a map down

A basis, here, is not a list of vectors but a coordinate system. A Basis n F V bundles a family Basis.vec of n vectors in V with a linear map Basis.coords from V to the coordinate space Fin.t n -> F, subject to two round-trip laws: rebuilding a vector from its coordinates returns the vector (Basis.reconstruction), and reading off the coordinates of a combination returns the coefficients (Basis.coords_linear_combination).

With a basis b1U,…,bnUb^U_1,\dots,b^U_n of UU and b1V,…,bmVb^V_1,\dots,b^V_m of VV, every linear map f:U→Vf : U \to V gets a matrix: the jj-th column holds the coordinates of ff’s value on the jj-th source basis vector. That is the definition, verbatim (Theory/MatRep.v):

Definition mat_of (f : LinearMap F U V) : Matrix m n F :=
  fun i j => Basis.coords BV (f (Basis.vec BU j)) i.

Nothing is lost in the writing down. The same file rebuilds a map from a grid — hom_of A sends u to the combination of target basis vectors whose ii-th coefficient is row ii of A against u’s coordinates — and proves the two round trips:

Theorem hom_of_mat_of : forall f : LinearMap F U V,
  hom_of (mat_of f) == f.
Theorem mat_of_hom_of : forall A : Matrix m n F,
  mat_of (hom_of A) == A.

Both directions are linear as well (mat_of_add, mat_of_scale, hom_of_matadd, hom_of_matscale), so the round trips and the linearity package into one isomorphism of vector spaces, vsiso_matrix:

Hom(U,V)≅Fm×n.\mathrm{Hom}(U,V) \;\cong\; F^{m\times n}.

The theorem you memorized as a definition

Blackboard. Let f:U→Vf : U \to V and g:V→Wg : V \to W. What is the matrix of g∘fg \circ f? Its (i,j)(i,j) entry is the ii-th coordinate of g(f(bjU))g(f(b^U_j)). Expand f(bjU)f(b^U_j) in the VV-basis — by the definition of [[f]][\![f]\!], its kk-th coefficient is [[f]]kj[\![f]\!]_{kj} — and push gg through the sum, which linearity permits:

[[g∘f]]ij=[g(∑k[[f]]kjbkV)]i=[∑k[[f]]kjg(bkV)]i=∑k[[g]]ik[[f]]kj.[\![g \circ f]\!]_{ij} = \Big[g\Big(\textstyle\sum_k [\![f]\!]_{kj}\, b^V_k\Big)\Big]_i = \Big[\textstyle\sum_k [\![f]\!]_{kj}\, g(b^V_k)\Big]_i = \textstyle\sum_k [\![g]\!]_{ik} [\![f]\!]_{kj}.

The last expression is the (i,j)(i,j) entry of [[g]][[f]][\![g]\!]\,[\![f]\!] under the row-times-column rule. Nothing was chosen along the way, so the rule is forced: it is the unique operation on grids that makes “write down, then multiply” agree with “compose, then write down”. ∎

Coq. The three blackboard steps are packaged once, as a rule for computing the coordinates of an image: they are the matrix, applied to the coordinates of the argument.

Theorem mat_of_coords : forall (f : LinearMap F U V) (u : U)
    (i : Fin.t m),
  Basis.coords BV (f u) i == hom_of_coeff (mat_of f) u i.

With that in hand the headline is one line. Take u to be the jj-th source basis vector and read the equation at row i: the left side is by definition the (i,j)(i,j) entry of the matrix of the composite, and the right side is by definition the (i,j)(i,j) entry of the product.

Theorem mat_of_comp : forall (g : LinearMap F V W) (f : LinearMap F U V),
  mat_of BU BW (linear_map_comp g f)
    == mat_mul (mat_of BV BW g) (mat_of BU BV f).
Proof.
  intros g f i j.
  exact (mat_of_coords BV BW g (f (Basis.vec BU j)) i).
Qed.

intros g f i j opens the pointwise equality of two matrices at one entry; what closes it is mat_of_coords for the outer map, read at the point f (Basis.vec BU j). Both sides of the goal are then the same sum by conversion, so no rewriting is needed at all.

Two lemmas carry the blackboard steps. lm_lincomb is “push gg through the sum”: a linear map applied to a linear combination is the combination of the images, proved by induction on the index type out of linear_map_zero, linear_map_additive and linear_map_scale. coords_vec says a basis vector’s coordinates are a delta, Basis.coords B (Basis.vec B j) == e j; it follows from Basis.coords_linear_combination once a delta-weighted combination is collapsed to its single surviving term. Expand, push through, collect — the three moves from first-year exercises — are each a named rewrite.

Multiplying two actual matrices

Over ℚ\mathbb{Q}, a field the definitions above actually compute in, the rule runs. mat2 builds a two-by-two grid from its entries in row-major order, and each entry of the product is one row-times-column sum a reader can redo by hand:

Definition MA : Matrix 2 2 Q := mat2 (1#1)%Q (2#1)%Q (3#1)%Q (4#1)%Q.
Definition MB : Matrix 2 2 Q := mat2 (5#1)%Q (6#1)%Q (7#1)%Q (8#1)%Q.

Example mat_mul_2x2 :
  mat_mul MA MB == mat2 (19#1)%Q (22#1)%Q (43#1)%Q (50#1)%Q.
Proof.
  intros i k; revert k; revert i.
  refine (fin2_case _ _ _); [ | intros i1 ]; refine (fin2_case _ _ _);
    intros; vm_compute; reflexivity.
Qed.

Equality of matrices is pointwise, so the proof splits on each of the two indices — fin2_case is the two-case eliminator for Fin.t 2 — and then each of the four scalar equations is a closed computation that vm_compute decides. 1⋅5+2⋅7=191\cdot 5 + 2\cdot 7 = 19, and so on.

What the isomorphism buys

Because mat_of is an isomorphism, a question about matrices may be answered on whichever side is easier and the answer transports. Associativity of mat_mul is provable directly, by the interchange of a double sum — that is mat_mul_assoc — or by observing that the product is composition, which is associative because function composition is, and transporting along mat_of_comp.

The derivative is where this stops being an aesthetic point. The Fréchet derivative of a function at a point is a linear map, not a grid; the Jacobian is that linear map written down in chosen bases. So the Jacobian needs no theory of its own, and gets none — it is literally mat_of applied to the derivative:

Definition jacobian {f : U -> V} {a : U} {L : LinearMap F U V}
    (Hd : is_deriv f a L) : Matrix m n F :=
  mat_of BU BV L.

And the chain rule for Jacobians, the statement that the Jacobian of a composite is the product of the Jacobians, is mat_of_comp under that definition — the proof of jacobian_chain_rule is the same one-liner, with the differentiability hypotheses carried only so the statement can be phrased in derivative language.
